Hong Gildong Meets John Doe

The placeholder names we test software with — Korea's Hong Gildong, a fintech's Kim Banksalad, America's John Doe.

Hong Gildong, met at work

Koreans have known Hong Gildong for a very long time — a Robin Hood-like folk hero — and for almost as long we’ve seen his name standing in as the example on forms at the district office and everywhere else. It’s a valid name; Hong is common enough that someone out there surely carries it for real. But it became the natural stand-in. A name loved by everyone, a name that inconveniences no one.

Later, once I was working with databases and doing all sorts of things at companies, Hong Gildong became my Korean test account without my ever deciding it. In dev and staging environments, with some plausible date of birth, an address, a throwaway phone number like 123-456-7890. There are broader common libraries for this now, but until just a few years ago we still hand-built the various cases. (The stories of open source versus in-house libraries belong to another topic.)

Then come the times you need several test people at once. One Hong Gildong isn’t enough; you have to manage male and female personas, so you fix the attributes up front and hit real trouble at the naming. Call it the pain of creation. Korean names carry constraints even on the number of syllables, so you can’t just make them up — and in test code, members of every famous girl group got summoned. Pick someone half-famous and the moment a teammate goes “who?” your productivity drops; an idol group you all happen to like made it much smoother. Of course, the moment you’re trading data with another team or another company, the canonical falls back to Hong Gildong. No way around it.

Kim Banksalad

After a long stretch in the US I came back to Korea, and for nearly two years from 2021 I was at Banksalad, a personal-finance app. Among the memories, one that genuinely impressed me was the name being used instead of Hong Gildong: Kim Banksalad. Looking back, being impressed by such a thing is a little funny — but it eased the pain-of-creation part a lot, and it extended naturally into Lee Banksalad, Park Banksalad, and, unexpectedly, they rolled right off the tongue. (Kim, Lee, Park are the three most common Korean surnames.)

It was intuitive enough that Kim Banksalad had long shown up as the new hire in tech-blog posts and as the letter-writer in the app’s money feed. So much of a developer’s life is the pain of having to invent a name for something; having an asset on par with Hong Gildong is, I think, a genuinely praiseworthy thing in running a company. Try improvising the same at other companies — Kim Google, Lee Samsung, Park LG, Jung Kakao — and every one is awkward in its own way.

John Doe / Jane Doe

I did search at Google for a long time, but somehow I never ran into this problem directly. Test accounts already started from a hexadecimal hash, the work wasn’t about handling individual people, and under a thick stack of rules — you either used a real Google account or could only see the outside Gmail accounts you’d personally registered — I never once felt the need for a Hong Gildong. Then, around 2017, working on the Nest store, I met it. A shopping site, essentially, and in exchanging protocols with other companies I ran into these names as the so-called industry-standard examples.

In the US and the English-speaking world, less constrained on length, John Doe and Jane Doe hold Hong Gildong’s position, with Baby Doe rounding out the set. It turned out to be a name that had long stood in as the fictional person across American textbooks, and one quite famous in database and legal contexts. Name a biblical figure directly and you can see the religious war breaking out inside the company; even a historical figure, and a person loved as universally as Hong Gildong is rare. Even a famous artist like the Beatles has its detractors. I saw it extend as far as John Smith and Jane Smith, but the names after that left no impression. It was a curiosity to me how naturally these ran through shopping and finance.

Appendix: test credit card numbers

The Nest store work was about implementing various payment methods, and I took the piece for BNPL — Buy Now, Pay Later, the equivalent of Korean installment or deferred payment — wiring up to the API of one such provider, Affirm. You lean on payment solutions, but to build the service properly, with all its flows, you need a range of test accounts and scenarios, Hong Gildong and John Doe among them.

The single most helpful thing here was the test credit-card numbers each card company made available. Numbers the whole industry uses in common, no contract required, and generous enough to manufacture assorted errors on demand — they made development enormously easier. A decade ago a search for “test card numbers” already turned up well-organized material, and lately Stripe, BlueSnap, and others have compiled excellent resources. On the small project I’m building now, I’m reaching for a Stripe integration without a second thought.

What surprised me, from the other side, was that when I tried similar work at Banksalad in Korea, almost none of this existed in public. Part of it starts with the overall information ecosystem of Naver and Google; part may be companies more conservative about exposing their assets — NDAs, test-account provisioning, rate limits. Before any actual work there was a great deal of contracts and paperwork, and far more constraints. Put charitably: a wall to one person is a field of opportunity to another.

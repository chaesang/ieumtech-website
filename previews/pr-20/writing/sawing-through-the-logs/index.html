Sawing Through the Logs

Sawzall, Sawmill, and a milldump run on a prayer — Google's language for log processing, when a thousand machines whirred on one Enter key.

Coming from the major, I carried an identity as a card-carrying programmer, and in school and at work my attitude was do anything, so I learned languages as the chances came. Today, one from the memory shelf: Sawzall.

Log

Even before joining Google I’d handled a fair amount of basic data — parallelism at the multi-core level, compiler optimization, and a step further, some graphics-processing techniques. I understood it more as a domain of patience than of skill, and I was still used to doing many things inside a single function. Joining Big Tech Google, I met, for the first time, the large-scale data I’d only heard about. Users’ actions — the kind of thing you’d leave with a printf and go check — were being gathered and stored under the name logs, and the starting point was to analyze them as far as your strength allowed and lay the next strategy.

A log is a felled tree; I read the word as cutting it along the grain and analyzing it one piece at a time — I was doing the saw I’d only seen in English books. The language specialized for this analysis was Sawzall (.SZL), the runtime was Sawmill, and I remember running the milldump command on a prayer.

Just as in C you can drop a printf anywhere to debug or to analyze later, logs went everywhere, service regardless — event logs, message logs, network logs — gathered neatly inside Protocol Buffers, and you’d read through them mechanically to run whatever analysis you wanted. Gathered up, they did look huge; but each one was usually one user’s precious flow, so I treated them quite seriously.

The unit function of parallel processing

Wikipedia has a simple example. Because the action is scanning the inside of one finely cut piece and picking out what you need, I never wrote or saw a very long one. Mostly it was looking for pearls in the sand, so what mattered was whether each condition matched — and the main habit was skipping the non-matches fast, reducing the nesting. And then there’s emit, the love-hate keyword.

The logic was built on the assumption that the same thing repeats countless times, and there was a rush when what you wanted came tumbling out — and just as much doubt. That I ran a language I barely knew and got the result I wanted… Later, studying MapReduce properly, I felt I could use it better, and right about when I wanted to really go at it, it drifted off over the horizon, replaced by the upstart language golang and a runtime people called Lingo (Log in Go). Golang belongs to another post.

Fire up a data-analysis pipeline and you often end up running about a thousand machines at once. I’d picture a thousand things going whirr on one Enter key, refresh the master instance over and over on a prayer, and — naturally (?) — when one or two spots errored out, I remember redoing the whole thing from the start, one at a time.

Kindred terms

I’ve lived in the US over ten years, but the actual sawing is still someone else’s daily life, the kind you see in movies. Translated to Korean it’d land near woodcutter. Staying in the field, the terms keep surfacing in related products and services, and each time I flinch a little — and am glad, because there’s something romantic about it.

  • Flume — from Hadoop, MapReduce
  • Logstash — the L in ELK
  • Lumberjack — a collector
  • Chainsaw — a log4j viewer
  • Timber — Android logging

Ten Blue Links

kPrism, result groups, and the long push to break ten blue links into universal search — with the fights over blogs and apps.

For a long time Google’s results page was known as ten blue links. Even now, all these years later, when it can’t show anything better, it falls back to that traditional form. The classic web-result UI carries plenty of meaning of its own, but to make that monotone into something richer, in more than one sense, we worked at it for a long time — and around 2007, when I joined Google, that work was at its height. Look at the results page of the near competitor, Naver — Korea’s dominant portal — and it’s made of many different pieces; from one skeptical angle you could say they don’t know what the user will like, so they prepare everything, and there was even a live debate over whether that counted as doing your best.

Wiping away the earlier mobile and embedded years and joining Google with a fresh heart, this was the first project I took on. Its name was kPrism. Prism — yes, the thing that fans out like a rainbow — and the k ambitiously attached to the front is the K of Korea. It was a decently large effort, split among eight of us, and inside it was the work of gathering and stitching diverse results together: universal search.

The challenges

For context, back then software engineers solved whatever problems came at them, so we did everything. In today’s terms the structure splits into frontend and backend: the backend built the full structure of the results it wanted to show, ranking included, and the frontend expressed that given structure well, laid it on the screen, and captured what the user did. On the frontend side — this was before Chrome existed, so we grumbled our way through Internet Explorer and Firefox, handling it stitch by stitch, and since serious JavaScript wasn’t in use yet, we stayed faithful to the basics of HTML and CSS.

Everything else was worried over in the backend. We introduced the result group — binding results into a tree to show them — and each vertical’s information got packed in to suit its situation. Groups get compared against each other; ranking between verticals and ranking among results within a vertical both become issues. The biggest question was whether to run each vertical on its own separate resources or let it ride along on the existing web. On a single query, news and images end up fighting the web, and if you force a group, are blog@2 and image@1 really being compared properly? How far do you prepare for the on-screen decision to stamp a group and say skip from here down? To look like a group you’d need to bind three… really?

kPrism’s needs got us in early, ahead of the result group; between our contribution to it and the project’s launch, a lot of good things happened. The verticals didn’t end up relocating separately, but the result group has been in use for a very long time since, and it was from around then that we said the right-hand area should go to something other than ads. It later became the foundation of today’s answer block and knowledge panel; anything clickable was treated as a result click, and the analysis pipeline distributed click credit accordingly. All of it, naturally, was unified and managed under the web’s index.

The effort for blogs

In Korea back then the material was penned inside Naver, and we put a lot of effort into freeing it — part of which was calling pages that met certain criteria blogs, setting them in a separate section, and trying to give them special treatment. It’s cooled off now, but at the time blogs were one of the prevailing trends, and the issues ran from checking whether the Korean web was even visible to Google’s crawlers, to how far to credit content that had copied the news, to authority questions, to what to do about the machine-generated material already rampant then.

Naturally, placing “Korea-only” value on where a blog begins and ends, it didn’t go well. We debated whether to run a site-level cluster as part of the web or to stand up a separate crawl and index. In the results, if there’s a separate blog block, should it be called something other than web? We weighed buying Blogger, Textcube, and the like. And near the end of that arc there was KNOL, which dreamed of being Google’s answer to Naver’s Knowledge iN — its crowd Q&A service. But Naver held on stubbornly and well, centered on Knowledge iN and blogs, so from Google’s vantage it didn’t work, and until the mobile world and the YouTube world arrived we had no choice but to concede defeat. They were the enemy, but I applaud them — and, facing today’s sovereignty moment, I privately wish they’d take on a bit more of a role.

The effort in apps

Then the mobile world came, and if those results were to appear mixed there, similar effort was needed. A new vertical being born and dying carries difficulty in itself, and the convenience and impact it creates for the user take a lot of thought. Here an opening appeared in a mobile world split into two great ranges. Search for “game” on Android and iPhone? It’s not StarCraft, is it? Shouldn’t searching “Naver” on an Android phone let the Naver app download?

The question of whether a separate vertical was needed, we approached surprisingly easily. On Android, the Play Store; on iPhone, the App Store. There were third-party markets early on, but by legal review they were negligible. On mobile, just hiding the rival’s store gave quite a good result. It was a natural marriage of traditional search and the mobile market, and since adding a specific site is awkward, it came out naturally as removing the sites that don’t help anyway. Unexpectedly we spent a lot of thought naming this — app or application, App or Application. Back then people were quietly calling a Chrome extension a Chrome app too; app/application gets used more broadly than you’d think.

Ranking was always a question mark. Should the query “game” give the same results on Google and on the Play Store? — a perennial. Many more questions followed, and to solve them a series of mobile-friendly projects picked up the ones below and carried them forward. Universal search, up to here.

  • As much as we work to find the official site, what should the effort to find the official app look like?
  • Shouldn’t we show something different to a user who’s already downloaded the app?
  • If the user deletes the app, how does Google Search know?
  • What about users who aren’t logged into Google?
  • What if there are two machines? What if many users come in on one machine?

2026 Cloud Solutions Report, #10: Inference Infrastructure (2) — Token Supply

Sold out before it is built, and 95% unused once rented. Reading the token factory's P&L has become the buyer's job.

Sold Out, and Sitting Idle

This report calls a facility that puts GPUs in a data center, feeds them electricity, and sells nothing but tokens a token factory. The last report was about the side that buys tokens from that factory. This one is about the side that builds and runs it, the side that supplies tokens. The 2026 token factory can be summed up in two numbers that point in opposite directions. A neocloud is a newer cloud operator whose main business is renting GPUs and which is not a hyperscaler. One of them, Nebius, said on its second-quarter earnings call that newly energized GPU capacity was being contracted as fast as it came online, leaving nothing spare in the quarter, and that it was negotiating short-term contracts at $40 to $50 million per megawatt [1]. That same spring, a report from Cast AI, a Kubernetes cost optimization vendor, put average enterprise GPU utilization at 5% [2]. The same GPU sells out at a premium on the factory side while, on the side that rented it, 95% of the capacity goes unused.

Both numbers can be true in the same year because a token factory gets paid when a GPU is sold, not when it is used. A buyer on a committed contract who leaves the GPU idle does not reduce the factory’s revenue. The buyer-side average is one tenth of the 40 to 50% utilization that the last report treated as the line between a dedicated contract and serverless, so what happens inside the factory does not stay with the companies building factories.

How to measure a token factory, and how to describe it, is still being decided by the company that sells the chips. At his GTC keynote in March 2026, NVIDIA’s Jensen Huang said that “every data center, every single factory, by definition, is power constrained,” and that “a one gigawatt factory will never become two. It’s physically constrained” [3]. NVIDIA describes factory economics as revenue per megawatt, with the weighted product of throughput and price as the engine [4]. Put in the language of the people building these places, power is the term you have to lock in years ahead, and tokens per watt is the term you can compete on right now with chips and software. The companies in this report divide according to which of the two they chose to lean on.

This report asks three questions. What does a token factory’s income statement look like? Why is everyone trying to leave their own layer? And by what path do those conditions reach the contract and the unit price on the buyer’s side? The report takes them in order: first the income statement, then six players and where each placed its weight, and finally the points where all of this touches the buyer’s contract.

The Token Factory’s P&L

Revenue, Cost, Profit

What this report looks at is the bottom two of the three floors the last report used to describe the inference industry: the inference infrastructure that squeezes more tokens out of the same GPU, and the AI data center that sells compute time. The two together are what this piece means by a token factory, which is a different thing from Token Factory, one of the product names used on the top floor where finished tokens are sold. The income statement reads in three lines. Revenue is contracted power multiplied by tokens per watt, by the price of a token, and by utilization, meaning the share of that power actually sold. Cost is depreciation, the practice of spreading a GPU’s purchase price over some number of years, plus power and interest. Profit is the difference. Of the four variables in revenue, power cannot be increased once the site is built, tokens per watt rises only by changing chips and software, and price is set by the market, which leaves utilization as the one variable a sales organization can move this quarter. The capital sunk into the building and the GPUs returns as revenue only to the extent the capacity is sold. In this piece, a factory’s utilization means the share of contracted power that is under contract and being billed, and a factory being sold out means that share has reached 100%.

What these lines look like in practice can be read off CoreWeave’s second quarter of 2026. Adjusted EBITDA margin, the margin before interest, taxes, and depreciation, was 59% [5]. Take depreciation out and operating margin falls to 5%, and once interest expense running at 25% of revenue is layered on, the quarter ends in a net loss. A little over half of revenue went to the equipment and what was left went to the debt. However good a token factory’s revenue looks, the costs decide the outcome.

Prices Fall, Factories Sell Out

Start with the price a buyer sees. Silicon Data tracks GPU rental rates daily the way a commodity desk tracks a raw material and publishes them as an index. In its October 8, 2026 values, the hyperscaler H100 rate is more than 2.5 times the neocloud-centered figure [6]. Over a longer horizon, the on-demand rate for the H100, the Hopper generation, has come down to less than half of its early-2024 level [7]. Yet the H100 one-year commitment index that SemiAnalysis publishes monthly, built only from signed one-year contract prices, rebounded about 40% from its October 2025 trough through March 2026 [8]. SemiAnalysis attributes this to delayed Blackwell deliveries leaving even August and September 2026 volume already booked. On-demand falling while contracts rise is the shape of the market in the first half of 2026. Table 1 puts the three indices side by side: the same H100 ranges from $1.70 to $7.15 an hour depending on where and on what contract you rent it.

Rising contract prices are the premium a sold-out factory collects. Nebius’s $40 to $50 million per megawatt is one example. CoreWeave said in September 2026 that short-term contracts of three to six months signed in the third quarter worked out to roughly $40 million per megawatt on an annualized basis [9]. Run the arithmetic and that is about $7 an hour for a Blackwell-class GPU, above the neocloud on-demand rate in Table 1. Prices fall and factories still sell out because generations turn over quickly: older GPUs lose value every time a new generation ships, while the new one is scarce enough to carry a premium in the contract market. So the buyer’s question, whether to take an H100 cheaply now or wait for Blackwell, becomes a question of where on the factory’s depreciation curve you sign.

Cost per Token, and Depreciation as a Floor

Convert the hourly GPU rate into a cost per token using InferenceX, SemiAnalysis’s cost analysis tool, and the width of the margin shows. Serving one open model on GPUs a hyperscaler owns outright costs $0.05 to $0.15 per million tokens on a B200 and $0.17 to $0.40 on an H100 [10]. A GPU lowers its cost per token the more requests it batches together, at the price of a slower answer for any one person, so the width of those ranges comes from the speed a user feels, and cost climbs steeply as you buy speed. The serverless selling price for open models, as the last report found, runs $0.1 to $1 per million tokens. The gap between selling price and cost is the token factory’s margin, and at the bottom where the two ranges overlap, that margin nearly disappears.

The other half of cost is set by accounting, specifically by how many years a GPU is written down over. CoreWeave and Alphabet use six years. Amazon moved some servers back from six years to five in early 2025, citing the pace of AI technology [11]. On one side is the claim that a GPU’s useful life is two to three years; on the other, the argument that a GPU keeps earning as it moves down from training to inference and then to lighter internal workloads. This argument does not stay an accounting matter, because the depreciation schedule sets the floor below which a price cannot go without reporting a loss. The cash costs, power and interest, are the same for everyone, so a company on six years can compete profitably at a lower price than one on five. That profit rests on the premise that the GPU really earns for six years. If the useful life turns out to be shorter than the schedule, the companies on six years are also the ones who take the write-down first.

Underneath all of these numbers sits power. NVIDIA claims that the Vera Rubin platform delivers up to 10 times higher inference throughput per megawatt than Blackwell, and about 10 times lower cost per token, for one workload it names [4]. If power is fixed, the way to raise revenue is to replace the GPUs on that same power with a newer generation, which means a token factory grows by generational turnover rather than by GPU count (Figure 2). But replacing a generation means the older GPUs have to vacate before they have served their six years, so this growth path runs straight into the depreciation argument above. That is part of why so many of the companies in the next section head for power first.

Margin Upstairs, Power Downstairs

Split the income statement by layer and an asymmetry appears: the potential margin is upstairs and the power is downstairs. That is why the token factories of 2026 are all trying to leave their own floor (Figure 3). Neoclouds buy inference API and software companies and move up. Companies selling tokens move down toward their own GPUs and power, as Together AI did in securing 500 megawatts. Chip companies open their own clouds and marketplaces on top of their own silicon, and hyperscalers build their own chips to pull the cost of the bottom floor off NVIDIA’s price list.

The four paths are not equally open. CoreWeave’s plan to acquire the data center operator Core Scientific and with it 1.3 gigawatts of power was voted down by the target’s shareholders in October 2025 [12]. The same company’s acquisitions in the upward direction, an experiment-tracking tool and a reinforcement learning startup, closed as planned [13]. Whoever holds power now knows what that asset is worth and does not sell it easily, so power has become something you build rather than something you buy. Which leaves the question of what token factories standing on the same GPUs under the same power constraint are supposed to compete on. Creating a reason the buyer cannot switch is also a way of holding onto that buyer.

Vendor Deep Dives

Six players, taken in order: three neoclouds, the chip company, the specialized chip camp, and the hyperscalers’ own silicon. For a reader in Korea the last section is the closest to daily practice, but the rest is worth following too, because part of hyperscaler capacity physically sits on neocloud sites, the floor under prices is set by the neocloud indices, and the chip company collects on both the silicon and the software. Each section starts with how that player sees the world and ends at the point where it touches the buyer’s contract.

CoreWeave: Scale as Collateral

In CoreWeave’s view of the world, this business is won on scale. Contract the power first, put the newest generation of GPUs on it before anyone else, sell that capacity long-term to large customers, and use those contracts to finance the next build, turning the cycle faster than the competition. At the end of the second quarter of 2026, backlog, meaning contracted revenue not yet recognized, stood at $104.2 billion [5]. The cycle rests on two anchor customers who commit to a large share of capacity on long terms. The OpenAI agreement runs up to $22.4 billion [14], and Meta has committed roughly $35 billion through the end of 2032 across two deals [15].

This factory has also stepped onto the floor above, launching Dedicated Inference, where the customer chooses the GPU and the serving software. Meanwhile the contracts keep getting longer: about 20% of backlog will not be recognized as revenue until after 48 months [16]. A token factory matching contract length to depreciation schedule is a factory proposing to share the risk of GPU lifespan with the buyer.

The weakness is that the cycle depends on debt and on exactly two customers. As of the end of June 2026, interest expense came to 25% of revenue, and 63% of first-half revenue came from the top two customers [16]. If either one moves to its own chips or its own data centers, that capacity has to find another buyer, and the interest keeps going out in the meantime. The buyer ends up in the position of having to worry about its factory’s customers.

Nebius: Winning on Tokens per Watt

In the view from Nebius, headquartered in the Netherlands, power and GPUs are commodities everyone buys at the same price, and the contest is over how many more tokens you can pull out of the same power. Its target for power under contract by the end of 2026 has been raised every quarter since November 2025 and now stands at 5 gigawatts [1], which is not power running today but the total the company intends to tie up by contract across many sites. Its anchors are Microsoft and Meta [17]. Where it parts from CoreWeave is in accounting: Nebius extended its depreciation schedule to five years in 2026 but still writes down faster than CoreWeave’s six [11], which means it books the profit on the same revenue more conservatively.

Where this factory places its weight is software. It took the phrase NVIDIA popularized and made it the name of its own inference service, Token Factory. In May 2026 it agreed to acquire the inference optimization startup Eigen AI, and in the same month brought over the core team from Clarifai and licensed its inference intellectual property [18][19]. The company’s explanation is that competitive advantage is shifting from the amount of compute to the inference efficiency that software determines, which in the language of the previous section is a declaration that it intends to win on tokens per watt rather than on gigawatts. On the buyer’s screen, GPU clusters and the token API sit in one console, so predictable workloads can be bought by the hour and exploratory ones by the token without changing vendors. The commitment terms still deserve a close reading.

Two risks run the other way. Having raised the contracted power target every quarter, the company now has to stand up more than a gigawatt a year from 2027, and a delay in either power or GPU delivery pushes the promise out. And most of its revenue comes from Microsoft and Meta, both of which build their own chips, so their silicon roadmaps are this factory’s demand risk.

Crusoe: Building the Power First

In the view from Crusoe, which started out mining bitcoin on stranded natural gas, the bottleneck is power. Microsoft’s Satya Nadella said much the same in a podcast reported in early November 2025: “the biggest issue we are now having is not a compute glut, but it’s power” [20]. Crusoe solves that problem from the other end, securing the site and the generation first and putting GPUs on top. In Abilene, Texas, after the campus leased by Oracle and provided to OpenAI’s Stargate [21], it is building a 900-megawatt campus for Microsoft with on-site generation of the same capacity [22]. Announcing a funding round in September 2026, Crusoe put total contracted capacity above 6 gigawatts, with 1 gigawatt live [23].

In November 2025 this factory added Managed Inference, selling per-token billing and reserved throughput, which gave it all three floors from the power plant to the token API [24]. What remains as risk is that a company still private has to lean on private capital to keep building at gigawatt scale, and that its anchor is concentrated in Stargate. What a buyer should check is whether power has actually been energized at the site where the contracted capacity will sit, and which assets under that vendor’s name are in fact the vendor’s own.

NVIDIA: A Chip Company That Wants Every Layer

In NVIDIA’s view of the world, token factories compete with one another, and whichever wins, that factory stands on NVIDIA silicon. But the NVIDIA of 2026 has built itself a position on every floor above the chip. In distribution, it placed itself between buyer and factory with DGX Cloud Lepton, a marketplace that gathers neocloud capacity alongside AWS and Azure GPU capacity on one screen for developers to choose from [25]. In software, it open-sourced the serving framework Dynamo to hold the standard, and sells NIM, the packaging that runs a model on top of it, under a paid license in production [26]. In silicon, the Groq technology it licensed non-exclusively for a reported $20 billion in December 2025 became the Groq 3 LPU, fabricated on a Samsung process, and the LPX systems that entered full production in August 2026 [27]. In model distribution, it filed in September 2026 for the $12.93 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, the repository where open models live [28].

NVIDIA is betting in particular on combinations of silicon. Paired with Groq 3 LPX, it says, Vera Rubin delivers up to 35 times higher throughput per megawatt for trillion-parameter, high-context workloads [4]. The stage that reads the whole input at once goes to the GPU, part of the stage that writes the answer one token at a time goes to the LPU, and the curve where cost climbs as you buy speed is meant to be flattened by the mix. While token factories compete on price, the metric, the marketplace, and the serving standard all belong to NVIDIA.

Widening across layers widens the risk too. Its largest customers, the hyperscalers, are simultaneously competitors building their own chips. The Hugging Face acquisition carries antitrust attention over the neutrality of the open model ecosystem. And the Lepton screen does not show which factory and which site the capacity a buyer picked actually sits on, or who answers when it fails. So the buyer’s questions are who the contracting counterparty is for capacity bought through a marketplace and who is accountable during an outage, and whether there is any layer of this industry NVIDIA is not in. If every layer runs through one company, switching vendors leaves the price floor and the software stack unchanged, and the switchable state the last report argued for is blocked at the chip company rather than at the token factory. The next two sections are about the attempts to find a way around that.

The Specialized Chip Camp: Not Standing in NVIDIA’s Line

In this camp’s view of the world, the GPU is an expensive way to make a token. Cerebras, which listed on Nasdaq in May 2026, took about 70% of its second-quarter revenue from cloud and services rather than hardware, which puts it closer to a token factory than to a chip company [29]. At the other end, Groq licensed its inference technology to NVIDIA and stayed independent, raising money in June and again in August 2026 and turning toward being a neocloud. The capacity it is adding runs on NVIDIA systems rather than its own LPUs, and its valuation halved over the year [30][31]. Among the newer entrants, Taalas, which etches a single model into a chip to make it very fast, was announced in August 2026 as an AMD acquisition [32]. d-Matrix, which uses general-purpose memory in place of the high-bandwidth memory GPUs need, entered production in June and acquired an inference deployment software company in August [33].

The companies that stay with their own silicon have a few things in common. They route around the two bottlenecks that constrain NVIDIA GPU supply, high-bandwidth memory and its packaging process. They keep a path open, as Cerebras has, to sell tokens through their own cloud in periods when chips are not selling. And they choose to sit beside the GPU rather than to displace it: AMD and Cerebras introduced a joint inference platform in July 2026 in which GPUs and wafer-scale chips divide the work [34]. The result is an income statement that increasingly resembles a neocloud’s. Revenue recognized from the 750-megawatt agreement Cerebras signed with OpenAI alone came to about 32% of quarterly revenue [29]. The risks resemble a neocloud’s too: Cerebras depends on two customers for 66% of revenue and on TSMC alone for chips [29], and a design like Taalas that fixes a model in silicon has to be refabricated when the model changes.

In front of this camp the buyer’s question becomes simple. How many of our workloads genuinely require generation speed measured per second, and what demand do they create? The answer decides whether this camp is a counterparty to contract with or a development to watch.

Hyperscalers: The Biggest Customer, Building Its Own Chips

In the hyperscalers’ view of the world, a large share of cost is NVIDIA’s price list, and all three have now moved their own chips up a generation. Google’s seventh-generation TPU, Ironwood, reached general availability in April 2026 [35]; AWS’s Trainium3 shipped in December 2025 [36]; and Microsoft unveiled Maia 200, an inference-only chip, in January 2026 [37]. What these three are aimed at is less external sales than pulling their own factory’s tokens per watt and depreciation curve off NVIDIA’s price list. Microsoft says Maia 200 delivers 30% better performance per dollar than the previous generation in its own fleet [37].

They differ in how open they are. Maia is internal only, Trainium can be rented by anyone inside AWS, and only Google has moved to sending its own silicon outside its own cloud. Anthropic secured gigawatt-scale TPU capacity through an expanded agreement in April 2026 [38], and Meta has been reported to be in talks to buy chips outright after leasing them [39]. At the same time the hyperscalers rent whole blocks of capacity from neoclouds to cover what they lack, which makes them both customers and competitors of the token factory.

Two things are worth a buyer’s attention. First, TPU and Trainium each demand their own software stack, particularly for training and custom optimization, so a workload optimized on them is harder to move than one on GPUs, and the buyer carries that portability cost. Second, nothing anywhere promises that the cost savings a hyperscaler gets from its own chips will show up in the buyer’s token price.

Sovereign: Token Factories at National Scale

Efforts to keep data and compute inside national borders go by the name sovereign AI, and the drive to build token factories is not only corporate. In July 2026 the European Union opened a call for AI gigafactories backed by €10 billion in public money and more than €20 billion in private investment [40][41]. In France, SoftBank has planned AI data center campuses [42]. HUMAIN, under Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund, agreed with AWS to build an AI zone in Riyadh [43]. Stargate UAE [44] and Microsoft’s Japan investment, announced with the explanation that it would help keep data processing inside Japan [45], sit on the same line.

In Korea, the National AI Computing Center broke ground on August 3, 2026 in Solaseado, Haenam, South Jeolla Province. At a project cost of roughly 2.5 trillion won, the plan is to install 15,000 advanced AI chips by 2028 and to grow that to 50,000 by 2030. Before completion, in 2027, some resources are to be opened early through private data centers so that startups, smaller companies, universities, and research institutes can use them through vouchers and discounted rates [46][47]. In the language of the previous section, this is public money securing power and GPUs first and opening the utilization above them to the private sector to fill. Whether the center will sell GPU hours or a token API has not been announced, and that decision will shape the demand that forms around it. The low utilization of enterprise GPUs cited earlier is the same warning for GPUs the public sector secures first.

Sovereign AI is an attempt to own three layers at national scale, but in none of these cases do the chips, the foundry, and the hyperscalers drop out of the picture. What a state can actually hold is the site, the power, where the data resides, and who operates what sits on top. The silicon is almost entirely imported. Serving software and models are the layer where a domestic share can be grown. A hyperscaler’s in-country region satisfies the residency condition but not the operator condition, so whether something counts as sovereign depends on which regulatory requirement it is meant to meet. And in places like Europe and Korea, where power costs more and sites are smaller, cost per gigawatt runs higher than at a global factory, which makes sovereign a product with a premium attached for the buyer.

Closing: Recommendations for CIOs and CTOs

If the last report argued for building a purchasing capability, this one argues for widening what that capability examines to include the vendor’s financial statements. A token factory’s circumstances enter the buyer’s contract along four paths: the order of capacity allocation, price and contract length, the vendor’s financial risk, and the premium on data residency. The recommendations follow that order. For organizations buying tokens through an API, the third and fourth apply first; for those contracting GPU hours or dedicated capacity, the first and second.

1. Verify capacity by site and delivery date

When a token factory is sold out, new customers go to the back of the allocation queue, and in a market where hyperscalers contract neocloud capacity in bulk, going through a hyperscaler can actually be faster. The trade is that it becomes harder to know which site your capacity sits on. An organization buying dedicated capacity or committed throughput is contracting for capacity that has not been built yet, so the first action is to get the site, the power energization schedule, and the GPU generation from the vendor in writing. A factory sells future capacity, so always allow for the possibility that the capacity in the contract has not been secured.

2. If you accept a long lock-in, get a generation-change clause

The last report’s advice to stay in a switchable state becomes, in a market of lengthening contracts, a matter of contract clauses rather than endpoint addresses. A long lock-in buys a lower price in exchange for carrying the GPU lifespan risk in place of the token factory, and a long contract signed into a sold-out market also locks in the scarcity premium. So if you accept a long lock-in, get it in a clause that you can move to a new generation within the same contract when Hopper gives way to Blackwell and Blackwell to Rubin, and at what price. The cost difference that follows from depreciation schedules, and NVIDIA’s claim of roughly a tenth of the cost per token on Rubin, are the grounds for that negotiation. Check whether your existing contracts already contain a clause adjusting price when a new generation is deployed, and prepare for the negotiation by matching contract length to the generation cycle or setting annual price step-downs in advance.

3. Look at your vendor’s top customers, and at their chip roadmaps

A token factory that takes more than 60% of revenue from its top two customers faces one of two outcomes if an anchor leaves: the freed capacity floods the market and prices collapse, or the vendor’s finances wobble and service continuity is at risk. Which one it is depends on that factory’s debt and interest expense and on who the anchor is, so read customer concentration, debt principal, and the identity of the anchors out of a listed vendor’s quarterly filings and put them in your contract review checklist. For a private vendor, read the public signals, the large contract announcements and the funding reports, and ask for clauses covering change of control, service discontinuation, and the right to move data and workloads out in a bankruptcy.

4. Pay the sovereign premium only where data residency is a real constraint

Sovereign is a product with a premium attached, and paying it on every workload does nothing for your costs beyond helping a token factory fill its utilization. Go workload by workload and write down whether the residency constraint comes from law, from a contract, or from habit. The more workloads that fall under habit, the larger the share that can sit outside the sovereign premium. For a public sector organization, that same distinction becomes the rule for dividing workloads between the national AI computing center and commercial clouds.

This report began with two numbers: token factories sold out before they are built, and 95% of GPUs sitting unused. That both appear in the same year means a GPU becomes a premium or leaves nothing but depreciation depending on which contract it is tied to. Closing out these two reports on inference infrastructure, the thing worth adding is that reading the token factory’s income statement, the document on the other side of that contract, has now become the buyer’s job.

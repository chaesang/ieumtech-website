2026 Cloud Solutions Report, #7: IAM/ID Platforms

Attackers don't break in anymore — they log in. Five identity platforms race to govern the AI agents flooding the enterprise.

Who Is That Agent?

Last month’s report started from a diagnosis: the AI agent has become the new consumer of the API. As agents began calling tools on behalf of people and reaching into company data, the API gateway returned as the control plane that traffic has to pass through. But if the gateway enforces through which path something enters, there’s another question that has to come first. Who, exactly, is the agent calling this API right now? With what permissions does it act, and who granted them?

Traditional identity and access management (IAM) isn’t ready to answer, because for the past twenty years the thing IAM managed was people. Single sign-on (SSO), multi-factor authentication (MFA), password policy — all of it is built on the premise that a human logs into a system. But in the 2026 enterprise, most of what actually accesses systems isn’t human. Service accounts, API keys, OAuth tokens, certificates, and now AI agents — non-human identities (NHI) outnumber human ones by tens to hundreds of times. Palo Alto Networks, which acquired CyberArk, cited a figure when it launched its next-generation platform Idira in May 2026: machine and AI identities outnumber humans 109 to 1 [1]. The NHI Management Group, which specializes in tracking non-human identity, put the ratio in its 2026 report at 45 to 1 up to as high as 100 to 1 depending on the organization, and noted that roughly 70% of machine identities are undocumented or unused for months while still holding high privileges [2]. Identities many times more numerous than humans, most of them over-permissioned, are alive and moving in a place no one is watching.

Attackers already know this blind spot, and in April 2026 the Gartner IAM Summit put it succinctly: “Attackers no longer break in. They log in” [3]. In a 2026 report surveying 5,000 people across 17 countries, Sophos found that 71% of organizations had suffered at least one identity-related breach in the past year, and in 41% of those breaches poor non-human identity management was named as a cause. The average recovery cost of a single breach reached 1.64 million dollars [4], and GitGuardian, which tracks secrets sprawl — the phenomenon of credentials scattered across code and configuration until they’re out of control — reported that in 2025 alone, 28.65 million hard-coded secrets were newly exposed in public GitHub commits, with leaks tied to AI services surging 81% [5].

The market shows this shift through big acquisitions among the large security vendors. The biggest deals of the first half of 2026 all pointed the same way: CrowdStrike announced in January that it would acquire SGNL, which has real-time dynamic authorization technology [6]; Palo Alto Networks completed its acquisition of CyberArk in February [7]; and Cisco declared its intent in May to acquire Astrix, a company specializing in non-human identity discovery [8]. It’s a signal that security’s center of gravity — long focused on firewalls and endpoints — is moving to identity. If zero trust has said for years that “identity is the new perimeter,” the 2026 acquisitions back that up with the flow of capital.

At the center of this transition, the major identity platforms compete, and this report looks at five with sharply distinct characters. Okta, the leader of the identity hub; CyberArk (Idira), the powerhouse of privileged access now acquired by Palo Alto; Microsoft Entra ID, the default identity infrastructure of the enterprise; and the two hyperscalers representing cloud-native IAM, Amazon Web Services (AWS) IAM Identity Center and Google Cloud. Each proposes an answer to one question. How do you rebuild a system made to manage human logins for an environment ruled by things that aren’t human?

Three Major Currents in 2026 IAM

The Explosion of Non-Human Identity — The Unmanaged Majority

Non-human identity isn’t one product category but an umbrella for identities of very different characters. Service accounts used for application-to-application calls, API keys for external access, OAuth tokens carrying delegated permissions, certificates that prove server trust, the executing principal of a continuous integration and deployment (CI/CD) pipeline, Kubernetes service accounts — all of it belongs here. What they share is that they’re usually made “just to get it working” during development or early operations, and then left behind, along with permanent permissions, with no one ever revoking them.

The problem isn’t only that these identities are vast in number, but that most of them are left completely unmanaged. According to a CyberArk study, only 1% of organizations worldwide have fully moved privileged access to a just-in-time (JIT) model, and 91% keep more than half of their privileged access always-on [9]. Permissions that should open briefly only when needed, and then close, stay open even when nothing is using them. Add the fact that a large share of non-human identities sit undocumented and neglected for a long time [2], and a leaked credential stays valid for a long time. The weight of the problem shows in the market size too: the non-human identity access management market is projected to grow from about 9.45 billion dollars in 2024 to about 18.7 billion dollars in 2030, an 11.9% compound annual growth rate [10]. However high you build the firewall, if tens of thousands of forgotten identities already sit inside it holding excessive privileges, the perimeter loses its meaning.

AI Agents — An Entirely New Kind of Identity

Compared with existing non-human identities, the AI agent has a different character. A service account repeats a fixed task, but an agent judges for itself depending on the situation, picks tools, accesses data, and decides its next action — it isn’t fixed in one place. It moves actively, like a human, but it doesn’t log in like one. The spread of the Model Context Protocol (MCP) covered in the June issue enlarged this problem: GitGuardian’s finding of 2,117 valid credentials in MCP-related configuration files alone [5] shows how loosely the keys an agent holds are scattered about.

Even so, most organizations are still at the stage of figuring out what they have. In the AI readiness index Cisco cited when announcing the Astrix acquisition, only 24% of organizations had the means to safely control the behavior of AI agents [8], and in a survey Okta cited, 69% of organizations said they suspected or had confirmed unsanctioned use of generative-AI tools by employees [11]. The signs of an incident are already appearing, while only a minority of organizations actually know who that agent is and what it can do.

At the Gartner Summit, the diagnosis continued that the target of identity management is expanding rapidly beyond people to workloads, agents, and credentials [3]. The very object of identity observation is shifting from people to agents, and the vendors’ response converges on one direction: don’t lay an agent on top of a human account or leave it as an anonymous service account — register it as an independent identity with an owner, a lifespan, and a scope of permission.

Security’s Axis Moved to Identity

What the security vendors’ first-half-2026 acquisitions commonly targeted wasn’t simple scale but integration — pulling identity into the center of the security platform. Identity capabilities like continuous authorization, privileged access management, and non-human identity discovery are being merged into one platform with network and endpoint security.

This connects directly to the platformization strategy of the security vendors covered in the March issue. If the March issue treated “how do you detect and respond to threats” from a post-incident view, this issue treats “who is accessing” with an emphasis on pre-incident prevention — and the two areas are now merging at the vendor level. In a structure where the security team and the IAM team move in separate departments with separate tools, this current gets out of step, so organizations should restructure where needed so that identity-based threat detection and response (ITDR) is folded into security operations.

The Five Vendors: A Comparison of Identity Control Capabilities

The five vendors are compared on five axes, as in Table 1: 1) discovery — does it make unmanaged non-human identities and shadow agents visible; 2) agent identity registration — does it manage an agent as an independent identity with an owner and a lifespan; 3) just-in-time, least privilege — does it remove standing privilege and grant only when needed; 4) dynamic authorization — does it judge permissions in real time based on the context at the moment of access; and 5) multi-environment applicability — is it free of lock-in to a particular cloud or ecosystem. The first three each ship an agent-specific identity product, while the two hyperscalers (AWS, GCP) build on SPIFFE (Secure Production Identity Framework For Everyone), an open standard that issues each workload a short-lived cryptographic identity. The difference: AWS leans toward strengthening existing IAM, while GCP puts an agent-specific identity as a first-class citizen on top of that standard.

Okta — “Every Agent Needs an Identity”

Okta is the leader of the identity hub, fronted by a catalog of more than 8,200 pre-built integrations. The breadth of integration, designed so that wherever you attach in the SaaS ecosystem you log in through Okta, was Okta’s moat — and in 2026 Okta declared it would extend that seat into the agent era. In Okta for AI Agents, formally launched in April 2026, an agent is registered in the Universal Directory as a non-human identity, given an owner and a managed lifespan, while its tool calls, authentication decisions, and access attempts stream in real time to security information and event management (SIEM) systems [12].

Meanwhile, the Agent Discovery feature unveiled in February 2026 operates as part of identity security posture management (ISPM): it detects unsanctioned agents, tracks OAuth consent flows, and converts discovered shadow agents into managed, registered identities. The figure Okta cited — 69% of organizations suspecting or confirming unsanctioned generative-AI use by employees [11] — shows the size of the blind spot this feature addresses.

Where it hurts. Okta for AI Agents is still a new product, so large-scale production references are few. Because non-human identity management is delivered as an extension of the existing identity security platform rather than a dedicated product, it can lack the depth of specialist tools. Enterprise-plan pricing runs high, a barrier for smaller organizations, and rebuilding trust after the 2023 support-system breach remains a task.

CyberArk (Idira) — Extending Privileged Access to AI Agents

CyberArk is a longtime leader in privileged access management (PAM), with its core capability being to isolate and monitor the privileged accounts that can control a system wholesale and to rotate their credentials on a schedule. When Palo Alto Networks completed its acquisition in February 2026 [7], CyberArk’s privileged-access capability became the identity axis of a Palo Alto platform spanning network and endpoint — and the fruit of that is Idira, the next-generation platform launched on May 12.

Idira continuously discovers identities, permissions, and access paths on an AI basis; enforces zero standing privileges (the principle of holding no permission in normal times and granting only when needed) and just-in-time access; and turns compliance from manual checks into automated governance through AI policy [1]. It’s a design aimed squarely at a reality where machine and AI identities overwhelm humans and standing privilege is left unattended — meant to prevent the damage, incomparably larger than with a human, when an AI agent holding standing privilege is compromised.

Where it hurts. Just after the acquisition, the migration path for existing customers onto Idira and the pricing rework on Palo Alto platform terms are still uncertain. It’s strong at privileged access (PAM) but trails Okta and Entra in SSO/MFA-centered workforce IAM, and PAM’s characteristic implementation complexity and cost remain a longstanding challenge.

Microsoft Entra ID — Widening Conditional Access to Agents

Entra ID is the enterprise identity infrastructure effectively installed by default in organizations that use Microsoft 365 and Azure. It’s often already in place, with no separate adoption to consider. Entra’s weapon is the policy engine called Conditional Access — the core zero-trust device that judges access in real time by combining user, device, location, and risk. In 2026, Microsoft extended that Conditional Access to agents.

Entra Agent ID formally launched in the first half of 2026. It registers an AI agent as a first-class identity and applies to the agent the same Conditional Access policies used for people, apps, and devices. The key is that adaptive access policy, real-time risk detection, and lifecycle management apply even to agents within a single zero-trust frame [13]. Third-party agents like those on AWS Bedrock can be onboarded via the Entra authentication software development kit (SDK) or workload identity federation. Microsoft said its Conditional Access optimization agent handles policy work 43% faster and 48% more accurately [14].

Where it hurts. Locked into the Microsoft ecosystem, its usefulness is limited in non-Azure, non-M365 environments, and visibility into agents outside the Copilot ecosystem has limits. Agent ID is still a new feature that needs more production cases, and the licensing scheme is complex enough that cost is hard to predict — a frequent point of criticism.

AWS IAM Identity Center — Strengthening Existing IAM Instead of a New Product

AWS IAM Identity Center is a cloud-native IAM that centrally manages access across many accounts, centered on AWS workloads. In February 2026 it introduced multi-region replication, so that access can be restored from another region even if the primary region fails [15], and IAM Access Analyzer evaluates policies across the whole organization to flag unused permissions and recommends least privilege on the basis of CloudTrail records.

AWS’s approach points a different way from the other vendors: rather than rushing out an agent-specific identity product, it chose to strengthen existing IAM and firm up standards. It adopts SPIFFE and the IETF’s (Internet Engineering Task Force) WIMSE (Workload Identity in Multi-System Environments) to replace static API keys with short-lived credentials in multi-cloud environments. Tied in with Bedrock AgentCore, IAM policy applies automatically within the agent’s lifecycle — solving the non-human identity problem conservatively but robustly, with standards and short-lived credentials instead of a headline new product.

Where it hurts. The price of that caution is that, unlike Okta or Entra, there’s no agent-specific identity-governance product — so if you want the same level of control, you have to build it yourself. Optimized for the AWS ecosystem, it trails the other two in multi-cloud identity management, and the depth of its non-human identity management is said to fall short of the specialist vendors.

Google Cloud — Agent-Native Identity Built on a Standard

Google Cloud put agent identity front and center at Google Cloud Next ‘26 on May 7, 2026 [16]. The core is Agent Identity, which gives an AI agent a first-class identity distinct from a person or a service account. Building on SPIFFE, it issues each agent a unique cryptographic ID and auto-provisions an X.509 certificate renewed every 24 hours; the access token issued is cryptographically bound to the agent’s X.509 certificate, so even if stolen it can’t be used elsewhere. Unlike a shared service account, each agent identity cannot be impersonated — the crux of the design [17].

After giving an agent an identity, the next stage is control — defining what that identity can reach — and Google Cloud solves it with a gateway and policy. Agent Gateway, now generally available, forces all agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool connections through a central path and blocks access to unsanctioned endpoints. To it, Identity-Aware Proxy is joined (in preview) to judge fine-grained access on the basis of agent identity and MCP context, and the forthcoming Unified Access Policy carries rules that even require human approval (human-in-the-loop) for sensitive operations.

Where it hurts. Where AWS leaned toward strengthening existing IAM, Google Cloud stands an agent-specific identity squarely on the same SPIFFE standard. But while Agent Identity and Agent Gateway are generally available, Identity-Aware Proxy is in preview and Unified Access Policy is still forthcoming, so the control stack isn’t yet complete. Cloud Identity’s enterprise identity base is shallower than Okta’s or Entra’s, and used standalone outside the GCP ecosystem, the integration burden remains.

A Closing Note for CIOs and CTOs: 2026 Identity Strategy

The 2026 identity strategy starts not with adopting a new authentication method but with pulling the invisible majority into the scope of management. A system that managed only human logins can’t handle non-human identities tens of times more numerous than people, plus agents that judge for themselves — but that doesn’t mean every organization has to bring in a new identity platform right now. If AI agents are already touching internal data, or service accounts and API keys are multiplying without control, or regulation demands a rebuild of the identity system, you should hurry; otherwise it’s not too late to start with discovery and inventory. To organizations that do need to rebuild, four recommendations.

Run the non-human identity inventory first. You can’t manage what you can’t see, and most organizations don’t even know how many non-human identities exist in their environment. Taking a full census of service accounts, API keys, OAuth tokens, and certificates — and learning each one’s permission level, last-used time, and owner — is the first step. Okta’s identity security posture management, or a dedicated non-human identity discovery tool, automates this work.

Register AI agents as independent identities and manage their lifecycle. If an agent accesses the organization’s tools and data, it must have a unique identity — not a shared human account, not an anonymous service account. Choose the path that fits your existing infrastructure among Okta for AI Agents, Entra Agent ID, and Google Cloud Agent Identity, but you must be able to answer three questions explicitly: who owns this agent, until when does it live, and how far does its access reach.

Apply just-in-time, least privilege to people and machines alike. Because a compromised agent abuses permissions faster than a person, standing privilege is far more dangerous in the agent era. Extend the just-in-time model — replacing permanent credentials with short-lived tokens and revoking permissions the moment a task ends — to both human and non-human identities.

Dismantle the silo between the security team and the IAM team. A structure where the two teams move separately inside the organization is out of step with the current merging security and identity into one. Integrate identity-based threat detection and response into the security operations center (SOC) workflow, and fold non-human identity management into security operations. For organizations in Korea there’s one more thing to watch: as the public-cloud security assurance program (CSAP) is reorganized into a single system under the National Intelligence Service (effective July 2027), items that overlap with similar certifications such as the information security management system (ISMS-P) are being streamlined toward exemption from verification [18]. Not only public-cloud providers but also solution companies that supply public institutions will have to redesign their identity architecture to fit the rules.

The opposite — what to avoid — is just as clear: bringing in a tool without an inventory; handing an agent a human account and running it anonymously; expecting that identity management takes care of itself just because a vendor announced “agent ID support.” All three widen the blind spot instead of illuminating what’s being managed, and backfire.

Attackers no longer break in. They log in. The starting point of a 2026 identity strategy is for the organization to know, first, who is logging in — whether that’s a person or an agent.

My First Encyclopedia

Wikipedia at 25 — the site I trusted most as a new immigrant, what it gave Search and the LLMs, and my regret over what Korean never built.

As Wikimedia marks its 25th anniversary, I came across the news of its collaborations with the major Big Tech companies. Mostly Wikipedia, but since I’ve used it from my search-industry days and before, I suppose I’ve been a devoted user for about twenty years — and it brings up various connected thoughts and stories.

From the user’s side

First, for a long time I felt uncomfortable seeing the banner that all but forced (?) a donation on every visit — so I’m relieved that worry, at least, has eased first.

It was the site I trusted and leaned on most in my early days as an immigrant in the US. I’d search on Google, and when a plausible Wikipedia page showed up in the results, I’d relax and read it top to bottom, learning as I went. Celebrity news, history, all kinds of math formulas. The sort of thing that made me think, ah, so this is what that encyclopedia somewhere in the house when I was a kid must have been. I studied English with it too, and the cited links were dependable as a bonus.

As of 2026 it’s still a mega-site inside the world’s top ten by visits. As much as people love it, the AIs loving it is only natural — and thanks to users like me, search engines like Google probably helped, so that’s natural too. That it runs on partnerships and a foundation, without ads, I still find hard to believe. Some sensitive entries get used as fodder for fake news, they say, but short of a particularly political topic, it was a site I read on trust.

From the developer’s side

Web pages, dumps, API access, even database exports — it offers methods so complete you wonder whether anyone should be this kind. Everything the English-speaking world needs is there, and though the directory structure is flat, once you find the URL and the document title, you navigate it naturally. Spreading a web page open and comparing it against a terminal is almost too easy, and the pages themselves are usually neither too long nor too short — though that, I’d say, is because I’ve been trained to it.

In my Google search days there was a daily dump inside the company that we used like a public good, and that flat structure meshed with Freebase, each reinforcing the other, serving as the foundation for Google’s knowledge base and knowledge panel. The information on the site was all so useful that I remember designing several verticals around it, poring over it hard. And of course, even now it’s used as the very first reference source, on top of Common Crawl, for pretraining the LLMs. If you were to build a separate search engine of your own, it’s without question the first data you’d have to use.

From my Google playing days

When information conflicted, it was often used as the source of truth that resolved it, and as a reference for good English. A no-Wikipedia index served as a solid baseline index, and whenever something was unclear, we’d ask Google Search or dig through the Wikipedia dump. The cited links carried meaning too — it qualified as a well-made, high-quality document and site.

Naturally it was among the first hundred sites App Indexing covered, and — unlike the web world — we agonized over how to handle the Wikipedia app, barely used in the mobile world. A case where the web was so well made that the app became useless. It was also the first site that mobile rendering, progressive web apps, and the other efforts competing within the search team rushed to handle.

For some domains I worked on for quite a while, like film, it stood in the position of public enemy. For the queries I hit most — “Tom Hanks,” “Forrest Gump” — I secretly hoped IMDb would rise, and agonized over how to beat that Wikipedia. And whenever I ran those experiments, without fail, users liked Wikipedia more. For the record, in Korea, Namuwiki — a Korean wiki — and the cinema sites rank higher.

The regrets about Korean

My eye is set on English, which makes it worse, but Korean content has long been lacking. In the period when I wished Wikipedia would become known and used by Korean users, Naver search flourished with Naver’s Knowledge iN and its onebox answers — and that’s tied to why Google-style search struggled then. Having taken part as a player and lost the match, I carry a lot of regret. With the methodology of the time, Wikipedia was also the best sample for weighing translation quality, since the same content is explained across many languages — and that’s connected to why EN-JA translation quality was higher than EN-KO.

Later, a bit differently, Namuwiki took this position, and though the words there were rough, Google’s search quality rose, and to match it my memory is that Korean Wikipedia’s content improved too. A relief, and now, apart from onebox and shopping, you rarely hear the complaint that its results page loses out. For the record, Namuwiki has a different license and doesn’t offer the developer-friendly methods above — a kind of private company. One you never know when or how might vanish… precarious, you might say.

In the recent sovereignty discussion, I feel a lot of regret about the space of well-organized documents in Korean. For English, painstakingly built, dependable data — Wikipedia dumps, the Common Crawl dump updated once a month — is far too easily accessible, while for Korean we only get to talk, little by little, about data synthesized on the grounds that it’s on Naver, it’s on the blogs. Once you’ve built a language model and an agent or a RAG setup goes to search somewhere, that too will spin the same cycle, and I suspect regrettable situations will follow. If not a foundation, then can the tax-funded or research institutions, or companies, take care of it?

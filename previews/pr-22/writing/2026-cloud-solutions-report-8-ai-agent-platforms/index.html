2026 Cloud Solutions Report, #8: AI Agent Platforms

Building agents got easy. Controlling them didn't. Six platforms, four adoption paths, and one question underneath: who holds governance?

Building Agents Got Easy. Controlling Them Didn’t.

In the first half of 2026, the center of gravity in the AI agent platform market quietly moved. For two years the industry’s question had been how to build an agent at all. Low-code and no-code builders matured, managed runtimes arrived, open-source frameworks poured out, and the barrier to building an agent dropped fast. And at exactly the point where that barrier fell, a question of an entirely different character rose to the center of the market. How do we trust, operate, and control the agents we’ve built? The governance layer that manages the agent became a hotter subject than the agent that writes the code.

Two signals from Gartner make the shift sharp. In April 2026 Gartner published the market’s first standalone Hype Cycle and placed agent development platforms at the Peak of Inflated Expectations. It had already warned that more than 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027 [1], and the causes named were not model performance but escalating cost, unclear business value, and insufficient risk control. A market receiving, at the same moment, both a peak of expectation and a warning of mass failure — that is what the agent platform looks like in 2026.

The line that building has gotten easier leads directly into the paradox that control has gotten harder. A significant share of organizations running agents in production have already had a security incident tied to them, and survey after survey finds that many can’t enforce even purpose limitation, the most basic control there is. Agents already call tools, reach into data, and carry out work on their own, while the systems meant to observe and constrain that behavior lag well behind. The gap between the convenience of building and the absence of control is where this report starts.

Looking back at the last two issues: June argued that the AI agent had become the new consumer of the API, and July argued that the agent needs a distinct identity and a managed lifecycle. This month’s report covers the layer above both — the platforms on which agents are actually built, run, evaluated, and controlled, and the ways of operating them.

Rather than listing vendors by feature, this report starts from a question CIOs and CTOs actually face. By which path will we adopt agents, and in the process, who ends up holding governance? From that vantage it examines six global vendors — Microsoft, AWS, Google, LangChain, CrewAI, and Salesforce — and closes by contrasting them with the strategy of Korean players, who are walking a wholly different road. This territory has only just opened, and no single vendor offers a complete answer; products and partners, models and integration work recombine every month. So the conclusion this piece arrives at isn’t about what to buy. It’s about what to fix in place and what to leave fluid.

The Core Paradigms of 2026 Agent Platforms

Before the individual vendors, three currents govern this market and are worth naming. In the first half of 2026 alone, dozens of major features flipped to general availability, and a comparison table of who offers what goes stale within six months. But the structure of adoption, the stages of the lifecycle, and the standards for communication move far more slowly, and they decide the choice.

Four Roads In: Who Holds Governance?

The paths by which an enterprise adopts an agent platform divide into four, according to where the control over governance sits.

The first is to lay agents on top of the cloud and productivity stack you already run. Microsoft Copilot Studio, AWS Bedrock AgentCore, and Google Gemini Enterprise belong here. On this path, governance is delegated naturally to that vendor’s identity, policy, and compliance infrastructure.

The second is to build it yourself on a neutral framework that isn’t locked to a particular cloud or model. LangChain with LangGraph, and CrewAI, are the representatives. Here the organization holds control outright — and carries the corresponding weight of implementing and operating it.

The third is to subscribe to a finished agent already integrated into the business SaaS you use. As with Salesforce Agentforce, governance arrives whole, together with the data and the work processes.

The fourth is a hybrid of the three: a neutral framework (LangGraph) running on a managed runtime (AWS AgentCore), with a SaaS agent (Agentforce) wired in over standard protocols. In practice, most of 2026 converges on this fourth path.

The reason the frame holds is plain. Feature tables go stale in six months, but the question of whom you delegate governance to is a structural choice arising from your cloud strategy, your regulatory environment, and your internal capability — and structural choices don’t change easily. The measure that’s actually useful to a CIO or CTO is not what a platform can do, but where you intend to put the center of gravity of control.

The Agent Lifecycle: From Build to Governance

Whichever path you take, an agent passes through the same four stages: it is built, it runs, it is evaluated, and it is governed. The practical measure for comparing vendors is how fully each of those four is filled.

Build is the most mature stage as of 2026, and it has effectively leveled up across the board. With low-code builders, frameworks, and the Model Context Protocol (MCP) for connecting tools all in place, whether you can build is no longer a differentiator. Runtime is maturing fast. Long-running execution — hours, and in some cases up to seven days — along with session and memory management and microVM-grade isolation, is being filled in as managed service through successive general-availability releases of AWS AgentCore Harness and Google Agent Runtime. The trouble is the other two stages.

At the evaluation stage, the market is absorbing a painful lesson: passing a benchmark is not the same as being safe in production. As the recognition spread that benchmarks measure capability but not governance, evaluation engineering has emerged as a distinct role. A large share of the reliability problems that appear in production were never predicted by any benchmark. And governance is the real battleground of 2026 — taking inventory of agents, assigning them identities, keeping audit logs, detecting anomalous behavior, and blocking actions in advance by policy. It is the least mature area, and the one where vendors differ most.

So the question to ask when choosing a platform is not whether you can build an agent with it. It’s whether you can evaluate and control what you’ve built.

Protocol Standardization: MCP and A2A as the Common Tongue

As agents began talking to each other and handling outside tools, the competition to set the grammar of that conversation was effectively settled in the first half of 2026, and two protocols took hold as the common tongue.

One is A2A (Agent-to-Agent), which governs communication between agents. Proposed by Google and backed by AWS and Microsoft, it was in production at more than 150 organizations as of 2026 and shipped SDKs in five languages [2]; Copilot Studio, Azure AI Foundry, and Bedrock AgentCore all carry A2A as a formal feature. The other is MCP (Model Context Protocol), which governs tool connection. Released by Anthropic, it has become the de facto common tongue for wiring tools and data together, and with every major platform adopting it as a default, this particular standards race has essentially closed.

Standardization cuts both ways, though. The easier interoperation between agents becomes, the more it matters to trace and control who called whom. The June 2026 MCP specification added recursive composition, in which a server itself operates as an agent, and the deeper the chain of connections runs, the harder auditing and policy enforcement get. So the prescription for CIOs and CTOs is to write A2A and MCP compatibility into the architectural requirements for any new agent system, while feeling no obligation to convert systems already in operation. That these standards settled means more than compatibility. It means the technical ground now exists for swapping out, later, which model, which partner, and which product you put on top. A design that fixes control in place while leaving the combination above it fluid becomes possible only here.

The Vendors, One by One

The six global vendors are examined in the order of the four paths above: cloud-integrated platforms laid on the stack (Microsoft, AWS, Google), neutral frameworks you build with yourself (LangChain, CrewAI), and the finished product you subscribe to whole (Salesforce). The focus stays on how each fills build, runtime, evaluation, and governance, and where each puts control.

Microsoft Copilot Studio and Agent 365 — Governance Laid Over M365 [Path 1]

Copilot Studio, Microsoft’s low-code agent builder, effectively completed its transition in the first half of 2026 from a low-code chatbot builder to a governed enterprise agent platform. Its strength is the depth of its integration with the M365 ecosystem: Entra ID permissions, Purview sensitivity labels, and Conditional Access policies apply to agents automatically, so an organization already on M365 gets a control regime without standing up new governance infrastructure. That more than 230,000 organizations — including 90% of the Fortune 500 — have built agents with Copilot Studio is itself an ecosystem of considerable mass.

The pivot of that transition is Agent 365, generally available in May 2026: a governance control plane for registering and controlling every agent across an organization. It aims squarely at organizations running multiple custom agents, and its deeper security integration with Defender and Purview is expanding through preview [3]. Late in June came a full engine-level redesign, bringing a workflow designer that puts structured steps and AI reasoning on one canvas, MCP server connections, and formal A2A support. Microsoft says the new orchestrator raised evaluation performance roughly 20% while halving token consumption [4].

Where it hurts. The licensing barrier went up. New purchases of Agent 365 require E5, the upper Microsoft 365 tier, as of June 2026 — E3 alone doesn’t qualify — which raises the threshold for midsize companies. And the M365 ecosystem that is the strength turns into the limit: integrating outside systems such as Salesforce or SAP still calls for a middle layer like MuleSoft, and complex agent workflows still ask for developer involvement.

AWS Bedrock AgentCore — Execution Infrastructure That Takes No Side [Paths 1 and 4]

AgentCore, the serverless infrastructure dedicated to running agents within Bedrock, AWS’s generative-AI platform, chose a strategy of taking no framework’s side and becoming everyone’s execution layer. Strands, LangGraph, the OpenAI Agents SDK, Google ADK, and the Claude Agent SDK all run on top of AgentCore, using AWS’s own security and compliance infrastructure — IAM, VPC, CloudTrail — as is. That framework neutrality makes AgentCore a natural foundation for the fourth path, the hybrid.

Progress through 2026 has been marked. Managed Harness, generally available in June, runs a production agent from a declarative configuration of model, tools, skills, and instructions alone [5], while the Policy feature intercepts agent behavior in advance through natural-language rules — “block approval when the refund exceeds this amount” — enforced outside the agent’s own reasoning loop [6]. In July, unified observability that gathers traces and logs into a single log group went generally available, extending managed coverage into the evaluation and governance stages [7].

Where it hurts. From a Korean adoption standpoint, as of July 2026 AgentCore had expanded to Bangkok, Malaysia, Milan, and Spain but not yet to the Seoul region, which forces latency and data residency into the calculation. AgentCore Browser and Payments remain in preview, and there’s residual unpredictability in how precisely a natural-language policy will be interpreted.

Google Gemini Enterprise — The Agent as a Top-Level Concept [Path 1]

At Cloud Next in April 2026, Google retired the Vertex AI brand and consolidated everything into the Gemini Enterprise agent platform [8]. The reorganization lifts the agent to the top-level concept of the architecture and covers the whole lifecycle in four layers: build, scale, govern, and optimize. At the build layer, ADK 2.0 — now generally available for Python and Go — carries a graph-based engine and human-in-the-loop natively [9]; at the scale layer, an agent runtime that runs up to seven days; at the governance layer, an agent identity built on CNCF SPIFFE. Each reached general availability. On infrastructure, Google leans on eighth-generation TPUs and direct access to more than 200 foundation models, Claude among them.

Where it hurts. The rebrand left confusion across existing Vertex AI documentation and APIs, and the burden of updating codebases along with it. The agent gateway and registry at the core of the governance layer are still in preview, and the complexity of enterprise governance is itself a barrier for smaller companies.

LangChain, LangGraph, and LangSmith — The De Facto Standard, Unbound to Any Cloud [Path 2]

LangChain, which began in open source, now sits as the de facto default of the enterprise agent development stack. Its greatest strength is flexibility unbound to a particular cloud or model, which makes it the choice when an organization wants to hold governance itself rather than hand it to a vendor. The three pieces are one stack from one company: LangChain as the general framework, LangGraph handling orchestration with stateful control flow for complex work, and LangSmith running operations with tracing, evaluation, and cost management in one place. LangGraph 1.0, in October 2025, was an answer to the enterprise’s long-standing distrust that open source might break at any time — automatic state recovery, human-in-the-loop built in, and a compatibility guarantee not to break existing code before 2.0 [10]. LangSmith Fleet, which operates agents in one click, ships attribute-based access control (ABAC) and tamper-evident audit logs, and the NVIDIA partnership announced in March 2026 secured an on-premises deployment path [11].

Where it hurts. The weakness is the flip side of the flexibility. The learning curve of the graph abstraction is a barrier for beginners, and the boundary between open source and paid enterprise features is unclear enough that it’s confusing to know where costs begin. Whether LangSmith’s deep evaluation capability holds up against specialist observability tools such as Galileo or Arize is still an open argument.

CrewAI — Role-Based Multi-Agent Orchestration [Path 2]

CrewAI is an open-source framework that models collaboration intuitively by defining each agent as a crew member with a role, a goal, and a backstory. Its low Python-based barrier to entry has given it wide reach; it reported roughly 2 billion cumulative agent executions over the trailing twelve months [12]. It is moving toward SaaS with CrewAI Enterprise, which adds serverless execution and a central dashboard.

Where it hurts. The concern is enterprise maturity. Governance features such as RBAC, audit logs, and SSO trail LangSmith, and the company’s claim of reach into the Fortune 500 — reported as nearly half [12] — is ambiguous about whether those are formal contracts or individual use, a figure to cite with care.

Salesforce Agentforce — A Finished Agent Embedded in the CRM [Path 3]

Agentforce makes its strongest case on the fact that CRM data, business process, and agent are unified inside one platform. More than 180,000 existing customers can deploy agents immediately with no additional integration, and that coupling is a moat competitors find hard to copy. It is the exemplar of the packaged subscription model, in which governance is delivered whole alongside data and process. The results back the model’s persuasiveness: as of the quarter reported in May 2026, Agentforce annual recurring revenue reached $1.2 billion, up 205% year over year. Agentic work units (AWU) delivered across Agentforce and Slack reached 3.8 billion, growing 111% quarter over quarter, and processed tokens reached 28.6 trillion [13]. Agentforce Operations, which puts agents on manual back-office processes, was added, and platform-wide latency improvements cut latency by 70%.

Where it hurts. The limits come from the CRM axis itself. Integrating systems outside the Salesforce ecosystem still depends on MuleSoft, which adds complexity and cost, and coverage is narrow in domains far from CRM — manufacturing, logistics, R&D. The price of the bundle that pairs Agentforce with Data 360, the customer data platform, is also called a burden for midsize companies.

In Korea: Not a Solution to Buy, but a Partner to Work With

Korean CIOs and CTOs carry a concern of a different order: the Korean market has no clear domestic agent platform product to pick up and adopt. What Korean IT services companies actually deliver is not a finished product but hands-on construction, working close to the customer’s site to solve that organization’s problem alongside them. This territory, long called SI work, is evolving in character toward the forward deployed engineer (FDE) model that Palantir popularized. So the choice in Korea isn’t which domestic product to buy. It’s whether to adopt a global product and operate it ourselves, or to run a close-in build with a Korean partner. The more an organization is constrained by network separation rules or data residency — which put global managed platforms such as AgentCore, with no Seoul region, out of reach — the more weight falls on the latter. And the firms often combine the two, with a Korean partner transplanting a global product into the site.

A Closing Note for CIOs and CTOs

The agent platform market has only just opened, and the enterprise board is being redrawn on top of it. Global products and local hands-on partners, cloud service providers and managed service providers, the side that builds models and the side that transplants them into the field, keep forming alliances and changing the combination. In a phase like this, what matters is dividing what to fix in place from what to leave fluid. Governance is the axis that doesn’t change whatever else you choose, so fix it first, and design the products, partners, and models above and below it so they can be swapped against whatever criteria you set. That makes the eye of an architect who designs the combination while holding control more valuable than the eye that guesses which vendor wins. Four recommendations follow from that view.

1. Vet vendors on evaluation and governance, not on build

Build has leveled up, so whether you can make an agent with this platform no longer separates anyone. What to ask is whether you can evaluate and control it afterward. Check for an inventory of agents, an identity assigned to each, audit logs, anomaly detection, and the ability to block behavior in advance by policy. Include evaluation engineering from the architecture design stage in particular.

2. Decide where your organization’s control sits before you pick a path

Choose the path by control, not by features. If you’re on M365 and comfortable delegating governance to the vendor’s infrastructure, Copilot Studio and Agent 365 fit. If multi-framework flexibility and self-governance matter, LangGraph on top of AgentCore. If CRM is the core data source, Agentforce. If you must avoid cloud lock-in and run on-premises in parallel, LangGraph with LangSmith. Since most of reality converges on the hybrid, put A2A and MCP compatibility into the architectural requirements from the start.

3. If you choose a partner, evaluate the team, not the product

As noted, where regulatory or sovereignty requirements constrain global managed platforms, close-in construction with a local partner becomes the practical alternative — and that is not a product purchase but a project run together, in the FDE manner. So evaluate on the team’s domain understanding, its capacity to carry a proof of concept through to production, its operational guarantees (SLA, incident response, model lifecycle), and references at comparable scale. Since Korean stacks adopt MCP as well, examine the interoperability path with the global stack closely.

4. Multi-agent is a means, not an end

The proposition that multi-agent is simply better than a single agent doesn’t hold. With clear inputs and outputs, a single domain, and execution inside ten minutes, one agent is enough. Multi-agent is justified only when the workflow is complex enough that parallelism is needed or role separation raises quality, or when execution runs for hours. Multi-agent raises complexity, debugging cost, and the risk of failure propagation along with it. Even now, with A2A settling into a standard, the justification for orchestration comes from one question: does this problem genuinely require several agents?

In the age of AI, in the age of agents, the alliances will keep forming and re-forming. But to an organization that holds control, a board in motion is not a threat — it is a fine set of options. To the leaders living through this complicated time, this time with so much more to learn, my encouragement once again.

The Dream I Buried

A Linux smartphone before its time, a Symbian job that burned me out on phones — then meeting Android at a Google all-hands.

Today’s subject is Android. It was close to a common noun once, and at some point it became that green horned thing — a mobile operating system that also owns a character. This is the first story, up to the point where I met it.

The Linux smartphone

This goes back a good while. From 2000 to 2004, at a company called PalmPalm Tech, doing my national service as a research-service engineer — Korea’s alternative to military conscription — I met the keyword Linux smartphone. There was a brand, Tynux; we built it like any other Linux distribution, attached a phone module, and pulled up the call stack, while another group bolted features onto a feature phone’s RTOS. My job was mainly the kernel and device-driver side — getting the hardware usable: pick the device, write the driver, make the APIs work, and figure the applications would sort themselves out somehow. On top of Linux we made a clumsy UI, poorly defined middleware, a not-at-all-pretty but more-or-less-working strange object that could make calls.

Prototype suited it far too well — a strange result, missing a lot before it could reach the market. Years later, if I started listing what it lacked back then, I could go on forever.

Symbian?

In the era before anyone knew the iPhone was coming, the world’s phones were split between the feature phone’s Motorola Razr and the twelve-button-specialized S60/Symbian. It was Motorola’s age in the US, Nokia’s in Europe, and Korea’s phones followed a step behind. Betting the coming smartphone age belonged to Nokia, I did Nokia/Symbian/S60 work worth a line on a résumé.

It was building Samsung Electronics’ Europe-bound S60 phones — Samsung the prime, its outsourcing partner Nokia the sub, and Teleca Korea, which took Nokia’s order, the sub-sub. With people who lived through that same era, I shuttled between the Gumi factory and the Suwon site, and barely earned one model on the résumé. Worn down and sick of it, with only a limited set of companies I could move to, I chose another path, resolving never to do phones again.

Then a company called Apple put out the iPhone — a phone as the next generation after the iPod — and I remember the faint regret of thinking, I suppose I’ll have to bury my Symbian career here.

The first meeting, at an all-hands

Having resolved to find a new life unrelated to phones, I worked briefly at a national research institute, and then, with an entirely fresh heart, got the chance to join Google Korea as an early member in 2007. A foreign company my parents had never heard of, but for someone from the major it was a good place to start over. I prepared hard for the interview, luck followed, and I passed.

New-hire orientation at headquarters ran three months, so I took my first business trip to the US — and midway through, in the form of a company-wide demo, I met Android for the first time. Positioned as the counter to Apple’s iPhone, and naturally, Andy Rubin gave the live demo. Linux at the base, a JVM on top, rich middleware, a clean GUI, support for an app ecosystem — I was looking at the finished version of the thing we’d dreamed of back at PalmPalm Tech, and a lot of thoughts went back and forth.

As the shock passed — the thing I’d been full of love and resentment for, the thing I’d tried to set on the far shelf of memory, flickering again before my eyes — there was something else. That the future isn’t to be thought of carelessly; that about the past, maybe it had only been too far ahead of its time; that there had also been real limits to my capabilities; a small reproach for a past where I hadn’t crossed the lines more fiercely. Ironically, it resonated in a way that suited starting over.

The last memory — “Why not Python?”

I don’t know if every all-hands, every open mic, was like this, but a lot of people lined up, and murmuring, they yielded to one person, and someone who looked at a glance — even to me — like a master asked the first question. “Why not Python?”

The one asking was Guido van Rossum, the creator of Python, at the time a very famous person building Python and internal productivity tools at Google. What followed, as I remember, was a good deal of talk about licensing I could barely follow — an issue that would go back and forth, all the way to the courts, for a very long time after.

That people like this gathered to have talks like this… what kind of company was I about to work for… I remember snapping wide awake.

Half of It Is Who Wrote It

Ranking never only read what a page said. Now that good ones are harder to find, the price of a single good one should have gone up.

The eleventh piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

Look a little closer at what search does, at ranking in particular, and the job is to order the world’s many documents starting from the ones you have reason to believe are better. What counted in that judgment, more than you might expect, was not only what a document said but who made it, where, and how.

Simplify a search ranking score far enough and it comes out as the product of two things: relevancy, how well the document matches the query, and popularity, how much weight the world gives that document. Real systems use more involved methods, but the roots were mostly those two. The best-known way of scoring popularity is Google’s PageRank, which gives a document value according to how much it is cited and linked from other documents, on the same principle by which a paper gains weight the more it is cited. Search engines then layered on what users did with the results, refining the popularity side over time.

Of everything ranking-related while I was at Google, my own favorite project was authorship. In an era thick with spam and copy and paste, it tried to get authors and sources to declare their credibility voluntarily, so that weight of several kinds could be placed on the original document. It approached the problem through web standards, a very Google kind of project, and the point was to lay one more layer of trust on a good document. I still remember the almost magical moments of smearing, in the broad sense of reading the connections among documents so they vouch for one another, and of anchor text, the words that describe the document a link points to. From the position of gathering evidence, the idea was to supply more grounds on which to judge each document. Documents linked from good ones benefited along the direction of the reference, and for a document that had little other evidence behind it, that became a meaningful popularity signal.

But a system that counts evidence this way has its shadow. Think about a query like who does Dokdo belong to. Dokdo is a set of islets Korea administers and Japan also claims, and the dispute is live. Suppose there is one Korean-language document on one side and ten Japanese-language documents saying the islets are Japanese. Suppose one of those ten is already well known. Intervening by hand on a query like that was something we could not do and did not do, on principle, so from where I sat the only thing left was to run the search myself, every so often, with my stomach tight, and then breathe out. What decided the answer was the documents the world had stacked up, and each time it left me wishing Korea’s effort to build content facing outward had been a little stronger, especially set against Japan’s.

The same thing is playing out again in the age of AI, unchanged. If what you take as the ground for knowledge, and for a model, is not documents joined by anchors and links you can follow back to a source but simply a heap of mechanically copied and pasted text, it will trip on fake news and misplaced authority sooner or later. The provenance of a piece, whose hands it came from and where and how, has to accumulate alongside the content. Plenty of services say they will handle this well. I am skeptical. Are they at it with the sincerity Google had back then? And if the sincerity were there, is this a problem that sincerity can solve?

Low-quality writing flooding in is nothing new, but now that producing one piece with AI’s help has gotten more than a thousand times easier, the scale is different in kind. Where one good piece once came with a hundred throwaways, one good piece now sits under a hundred thousand bad ones, which is to say the garbage grows far faster than the good. Picking out what is meaningful from that heap and putting it where users can see it is a burden the side shipping the service has to carry, and as the volume swelled the weight of that responsibility swelled with it. Even just storing it, reading it, and throwing it away costs a thousand times what it used to.

I respect the craft of people who carry writing and knowledge across responsibly. Influencers, communicators, many of them doing it in good faith, but there is always some private interest riding along, so when I meet new information now I weigh not only what it says but who is passing it on and why. A YouTube short that reaches me flipped left to right, or from a channel that looks off, draws more suspicion. One of the old occupational habits, probably. One more place to pay attention.

The authorship program of that era rode on Google+, so when that wound down the program ostensibly folded with it. But the approach, sorting for the good by asking who made a thing, where, and how, carried on quietly for years afterward through smearing and other techniques for minding search quality. Looking at it now, when telling what is real and trustworthy has gotten this hard, that old project seems like homework to be taken out again, and taken out larger. In an age when machines that claim to have read every document people made are pouring out new ones, the Dokdo question from before has gotten far more serious. The harder it gets to pick things out, the more one high-quality document should be worth. What worries me is that we may be going numb to that quality and that worth, and that the services may be learning it from us.

Stars Don't Travel Well

Giving them, receiving them, and having to interpret them. The same five stars said something different each time.

The twelfth piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

Back when Uber was new in the United States, a driver said this to me as I was getting out of the car: if you liked the service, give me five; if you didn’t, give me four. Out of five, four is a failing grade. Star ratings look like one scale the whole world shares.

I should say first that I am not a consistent reviewer myself. My mood gets into it, I read other people’s reviews closely and almost never leave my own, and on a place like Yelp or Airbnb where my name is attached I swing between five and one without any of the care it would take to split three from four. When the company that installed my internet asked me to rate them from zero to ten, unless we had actually fought, I gave full marks out of habit. If a restaurant disappointed me I said so by tipping less, not by going home and writing it up. Five notches or eleven, in my hands the scale was really only two, yes or no. A binary creature, apparently.

It varies a good deal by domain, by neighborhood so to speak. For films there are systems where the distribution spreads wide, the IMDb score and the Rotten Tomatoes rating. Amazon and Google Maps run on five stars. Then there is the world of mutual review, Uber and Airbnb, where the two sides rate each other, and that is where the extreme upward compression settled in, the one my driver was describing, where four is effectively a fail. Food and app reviews are their own case again: the same five stars, with generous and stingy standards varying from one neighborhood to the next.

Cross a border and the gap widens. If five is the baseline in the U.S. and four registers as a complaint, in Korea the rating became a channel for talking: give one star when something is wrong, change it to five once it gets fixed. Among ourselves, a five-star rating with harsh words in the body of the review is nothing unusual. The act of rating was tied to a country’s culture, not to its language. For the same restaurant reviews, the average and the distribution in Korea and Japan were not just different but wildly so. The spread is not limited to food. The same film, carried across a border, drew a noticeably different average and shape.

What interested me was how much the total score itself weighed. On Tabelog in Japan or Yelp in the U.S., the score the site assigns carries real authority, and a place in the mid-threes is already notable. In Korea, individual reviews poured in but the aggregate never gained that kind of weight, because there was a psychological barrier to leaving a restaurant review at all. Few people return to a place they disliked just to take a star off it, so the total naturally drifted upward.

Stand in the receiving seat rather than the giving one and the story changes. In the world of mutual review the user is also being reviewed, and getting four stars stings more than it should. During my years teaching as an adjunct at KAIST, a science and engineering university in Korea, course evaluations arrived at the end of each term. I had plenty of shortcomings and I braced for it. The ratings came to me flattened into a single average. The individual comments came one by one and got read in full. Discontent that had turned into sentences lasted far longer. How many out of the whole class mattered very little, and remembering that Blind, an anonymous workplace app widely used in Korea, and interview-review sites generally run heavier on complaints than praise was a small comfort. Still, the scale I used carelessly in two notches when I was giving leaves one empty star behind like a bruise when I am the one receiving.

For the developers who were our customers on the Play Store, that bruise was both daily life and the job. An app’s review section was frequently used as an official communication channel. Even with a dedicated forum or community set up outside the store, people figured whatever needed saying would reach you through the reviews anyway, so the teams worked that section hard. Difficult customers turned up often enough, and just as influencers in food delivery and restaurant reviews lean on their rating and write-up to demand unreasonable service, there were people who brought a star rating to the app store as a bargaining chip. I remember working with developers to sort those out. Thinking of all the customer success and customer support people I crossed paths with over the years, I find myself quietly rooting for them.

Five notches of stars: a binary choice made carelessly by the one giving, a single empty star that wounds the one receiving, a bargaining chip to a developer. They were saying different things from every seat and in every country. And once a system has to take in those uneven voices and do something with them, the side interpreting what that one simple star means has considerably more to work out than it looks.

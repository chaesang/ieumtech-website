The Detour Through English

Cross-language retrieval at Google Korea — translate the query to English, search, translate the results back. Why it came too early.

Talking with the various AIs these days, a lot of tools quietly go off (?) to English and come back, like this. Gemini probably asks Google; where Claude asks, I’m curious about. But if it lays the query out that considerately, it doesn’t have to be Google. The question was scenarios for the US being eliminated at the 2026 WBC — and the sites showing up here still look a way off from the long tail.

This method of going off to English and back is a technique used for a long time, called CLIR — cross-language information retrieval — and today’s subject is the record and memory of when we tried to implement it directly in Google Search. It’s one of the “memory” pieces, but with a spoonful of what I’ve been thinking lately.

Why CLIR in search?

In the early days of Korean search I did the CLIR project fairly seriously with colleagues. Quality documents in Korean were rather scarce then, so we thought we might show the relatively abundant, good English documents in the results — and placed as part of universal search, it could hold up as a meaningful vertical. To implement it, each query had to run through the following in order:

  1. Translate the Korean query into English.
  2. Search Google in English.
  3. In universal search, compare against the other results; show it if it’s better.
  4. Translate the top three or so web-result UIs (title and snippet) back into Korean.

Swapping the whole page was a feature you could dig out of some advanced menu if you really tried, but how far you could place the translated results among the originals needed experiments. For this to work meaningfully as a project, three things had to hold: coverage/impact had to be high, an improvement in result quality had to be expected, and the added cost had to be meaningfully small (?). All three were top-difficulty problems at the time, so it went less well than hoped. The various techniques we used along the way found use in many places, but the feature itself quietly folded, having gathered plenty of results in experiments.

Challenge: coverage

Becoming part of universal search brings a common worry: you first have to tell, from the query, whether to fire this logic at all. For CLIR the query was Korean, and we had to catch the ones that would have been better searched in English. In the pre-mobile desktop days, Korean Google had more English queries than you’d think — up to about half — but Naver did its job well, and Google was used heavily on the academic side, so among ordinary queries it was hard to find ones worth actually translating and comparing.

Headquarters judged, on this basis, that Koreans were a people who used English very well. And the hard-won academic queries were often findable directly in English rather than asked in Korean — cases where the user could type in English themselves, and where it collided with Google Scholar’s results anyway. Apart from a little use in specialized academic territory, what qualified was mostly foreign actors’ names typed in Korean, or foreign news; deciding, from the query alone, how often to run this logic, we couldn’t land on a number that satisfied everyone.

Challenge: quality

You can speak of quality at many layers, but intuitively: the quality of the query translation, of the search, of the result translation — that has to beat the quality of the original search results to be worth anything. Even at 90 percent each, three multiplications already put you around 70 percent, with more stories tucked in between. Even now translation quality doesn’t reach 100 percent; after GNMT in 2016 it was said to reach around 60 percent, so at the time we ran the experiments on a prayer.

And after all that effort going off (?) to English, if the result for the Korean query was already decent, there was nothing to compare against. A blog that had kindly translated and analyzed it in advance, or Korean news that had covered the foreign report first — those qualified, and to the user it amounted to translated by Google, so even when a genuinely good foreign site came, it lost out to the awkward Korean translation. A result loved in the English-speaking world, hoping for the user’s click — high difficulty.

Challenge: added cost

Because it was an experimental project attempted mostly in Korea, there was freedom in many respects, but thinking about it globally the biggest thing was user latency. One Korean-to-English translation, plus several English-to-Korean translations, had to be added to the critical path — and you had to decide whether to do it on every query or only on select ones. Getting slower was unavoidable; the question was how much. And putting in an if condition is itself an added cost — spreading the cost across all the users unrelated to this query, gravely, across the whole world’s users.

Unconsciously we agonized over translating to English, but the actually needed case isn’t always that. In certain domains Japanese would be better, and whether to compare everything against English, or to blindly throw comparisons across a hundred languages, became the issue — the reason it couldn’t reach a global official service. English served as the baseline from which many languages built the ground for translation, but the language-translation model and the amount of content are problems to be solved differently.

What I’ve been thinking lately

In today’s changed world after ChatGPT, I feel something like a jolt across the years here. However long it takes, users are waiting quite patiently. For a simple query too, they seem to have ten seconds of patience. Translating to English, searching, translating back to Korean — they accept it quite naturally, when at the time a stray millisecond made us flinch and shave it down stitch by stitch. In one light that effort feels futile now. Maybe we tried too early for the era; put kindly, maybe you could call it romance.

When Korea talks about sovereignty, this CLIR story comes to mind and I worry, quite seriously. As of 2026, with translation already a solved problem, Korean-language content will have to fight and win against what’s been translated from English — set apart from language itself. The nation’s official documents, the things created in Korean, will have to keep their line alive. From the anxious care of someone who’s watched the industry rise and fall, I have a wish that the people in government and Korean companies would take this matter a little more heavily.

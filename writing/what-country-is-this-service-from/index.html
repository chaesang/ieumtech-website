What Country Is This Service From?

YouTube once blocked uploads if your country was set to Korea. Which country a service belongs to was never as simple as its domain.

The third piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

There was a stretch when setting your country to Korea on YouTube meant you could neither upload a video nor leave a comment. Switch the setting to Global and it opened up again. The cause was a 2009 Korean rule people called the real-name internet law, formally the limited identity verification system.

What the rule required was that anyone posting to a bulletin board on a service above a certain size had to verify their real identity first. It was local law, and a company operating in Korea had to follow it, so Google tried to comply as I remember it. This was before the mobile era, and Google+ was being pushed with some ambition and would become its own embarrassment later, so the problem wasn’t really real names. It was how to get people to log in at all. Handing a difficult homework assignment to a guest you’d worked hard to bring in the door. In the end YouTube chose to block posting and uploading for users set to Korea.

Even inside one company the situation differed by product. Google Search, where users don’t create comments or content, slipped past the rule. But Gmail, which people used as part of daily life, raised a harder question. Gmail isn’t a bulletin board, and Google was running dozens of products at the time, so putting every feature through legal review one by one wasn’t workable. I was on Search then, so I had no direct or indirect responsibility for YouTube, and I remember watching these discussions go back and forth internally with a certain sadness. Around the same time Google Search was pulling out of China, so this was a period when the company spent a long while caught between local law and global policy. The Korean rule was struck down as unconstitutional in 2012 and disappeared, and since nearly every service now starts with a login of some kind, it may read as an old story.

The biggest locale question I carried then was this one. What country does this service belong to? Notice what the YouTube setting keyed on. It wasn’t Korean the language, it was Korea the country, and it wasn’t touching the user’s preference so much as tying the scope of the service to a country, which is to say to the reach of a law. Whether “the nationality of a service” is the right name for that, I’m not sure, but I haven’t found a better one.

An address like google.co.kr made everyone think of Korea and made local law easy to apply, but a domain never guaranteed a country. You can’t readily obtain the domain of every country in the world, and these days a country’s domain gets borrowed for an entirely different purpose, as with .tv and .io.

Even within one company the practice varied. YouTube used youtube.com/kr, Google used google.co.kr, Gmail used gmail.com. Looking back it was inefficient enough that a large company-wide project to consolidate everything onto google.com ran over the following years, and google.co.kr was left as a parking domain that forwards to google.com.

Set domains aside and the nationality of a service still doesn’t resolve into one legal answer. The question surfaces every time a commission dispute flares up around Google. It surfaces when people ask which country’s law applies to a service plainly hosted abroad. It surfaces when you ask how much meaning a physical server location carries in a cloud world, and again when a service doesn’t match the corporate entity behind it. Quite a few places need a definition of “country,” and once money moves through them it gets considerably more complicated.

In the days of “Beat Naver,” one of the team’s objectives was to grow query volume on google.co.kr. I understood the impulse to lift a single-digit share, but the moment the goal was pinned to the co.kr domain the arithmetic started to drift. People living in Korea who went to google.com and searched in English became invisible inside that target. We told ordinary users to use google.co.kr while, ironically, walking individual people through switching to google.com when they didn’t like what they saw.

Users now cross that boundary without a second thought when they need to. What used to be flipping a country setting to Global is now done with a paid or free VPN, far more commonly and within a fairly legal range. While I’m in Korea I can’t reach certain U.S. university sites, the DMV, or my insurance company, and from the U.S. I can’t see Coupang Play. World Cup season adds another round of hide-and-seek.

With sovereignty now a keyword, the nationality of a service doesn’t divide cleanly into domains and settings and numbers, and services keep adjusting against the people routing around them while trying to hold their own quality together. Settling on one country for a service is hard enough to grasp. Lay the country and language of the person using it on top, and then the country and language of the content it displays, and the difficulty climbs another level. Country settings happen in more than one place.

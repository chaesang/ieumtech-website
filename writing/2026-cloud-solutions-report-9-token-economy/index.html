2026 Cloud Solutions Report, #9: Inference Infrastructure (1) — The Token Economy

The bill arrives before the governance dashboard. Buying tokens is now procurement: where you buy, how you buy, and at what price.

The Bill Comes First

The month after an organization puts an agent into production, the first thing it receives is not a governance dashboard. It’s an invoice. Last month’s report concluded that building an agent had gotten easy and that the remaining problem was control, and in practice that control problem shows up first as cost. To produce a single result, an agent plans, calls tools, reads what comes back, and if necessary returns to the start and tries again. Stack that up and the industry reckons it consumes ten to thirty times the tokens the same work would take as a one-shot query. During the pilot phase this cost was a rounding error. In not much time it became a standing operating expense that has to be managed as its own budget line.

The clearest statement of this shift, in the industry’s own language, came from a company that sells chips. At GTC 2026 in March, NVIDIA’s Jensen Huang redefined the data center as an “AI factory”: the raw inputs are electricity and data, the finished product coming off the line is tokens, and productivity is measured in tokens per watt [1]. A chip company describing itself as a token production infrastructure business means tokens have started to be treated as a standardized commodity. Translated into the buyer’s language, inference is no longer first a question of technology selection. It is a question of procurement.

In its August 2026 forecast, Gartner projected that spending on AI-optimized infrastructure services (IaaS) would reach $42.3 billion in 2026, up 96% year over year, and that within it inference would reach $23.3 billion, passing training’s $19.0 billion for the first time at 55% of the total [2]. Corporate budgets show the same direction. a16z, surveying 100 enterprise CIOs across 15 industries, found leaders expecting their large language model (LLM) budgets to grow roughly 75% over the following year [3].

This report is about the side that buys those tokens. The inference industry is stacked in three layers, and this piece covers the top one, where CIOs and CTOs actually open their wallets; the circumstances of the side that builds the factories follow in the next issue. The questions here are three. Where do you buy, how do you buy, and at what price? What’s different in 2026 is that an organization can’t settle these three once and be done, because software has begun to take over from people the job of re-deciding all three every time an application or an agent calls a model. The state of being able to answer these questions — knowing what you use and how much, and being able to switch if you need to — is what this piece calls purchasing capability, and how to acquire it is where this report lands.

Where Tokens Come From, and How They Are Sold

The Three Layers

The bottom layer is the AI data center, a name that covers both the operators who hold land and power and the operators who put GPUs on top of that and sell compute time. In factory terms, this is the building and the plant. Above it, inference infrastructure is the layer of serving engines and optimizations that squeeze more tokens out of the same GPU, which makes it the production line. The top layer, the token factory, sells the finished product through an API. What NVIDIA calls an AI factory is all three together; the token factory in this piece means only that top layer. The three are boundaries of function rather than of corporate ownership, so one company may hold two layers, and a buyer who puts its own GPUs down and runs open models — the self-hosted route — takes the middle layer onto itself. And on top of this three-layer stack, a new tier has been added: a buying desk that picks which provider to buy from every time a model is called.

The three layers meet at a single point, and that point is utilization. A data center’s capital expenditure is a cost and nothing else until inference infrastructure keeps the GPUs running and converts them into tokens, which is why the margin in this industry can be larger in the upper layer that sells tokens than in the lower one that rents GPU hours. But that is a potential margin, realized only when utilization is filled, and the pressure of commoditization — where the thing becomes identical wherever you buy it and competition collapses to price — keeps grinding it down. For the buyer this reads as a choice. Buy tokens and you never think about whether a GPU is idle, but you pay someone else’s margin along with it. Buy GPU hours and you cut that margin out, but utilization becomes your problem.

Three Ways to Buy: A Taxi, a Chartered Bus, a Car of Your Own

In practice there are three ways to buy inference: the metered taxi (a serverless API), the bus you charter by the hour (a dedicated endpoint), and the car you buy and drive yourself (self-hosting). What separates them is what you hand to someone else and what you carry yourself — though even the taxi comes with a tokens-per-minute cap, which is a capacity contract in all but name.

The line between the three modes is drawn by utilization, meaning how much of the throughput you secured you actually used. One analysis puts the break-even between serverless and a dedicated endpoint somewhere around 40 to 50% GPU utilization [4]. For self-hosting, counting only the GPU rental cost, the break-even falls between 22 and 48% depending on how fast a response you need [5], and adding operating costs pushes that boundary higher. The same analysis calculates that a nearly idle self-hosted GPU can cost two to four times more than serverless [5], and the gap comes from staff and from the spare capacity you hold for peaks. Open-source serving engines have lowered the entry barrier — vLLM has become the de facto standard, and SGLang made its name on prefix reuse (RadixAttention) — but setting quantization levels, tuning parallelism and context cache (KV cache) memory, and recovering from failures still take dedicated people.

That boundary also shifts enormously depending on which serverless price you compare against. As the pattern of many providers serving the same open model took hold, the per-million-token price between the cheapest and most expensive provider of one widely used open model differs by more than eight times. Generation speed varies just as much, and price and speed are not proportional: the fastest provider is not the most expensive one [6]. A single line on a price sheet, looked at closely, splits into input and output, cache hits, and long-context tiers, and reasoning models — the ones that work through steps before answering — bill the thinking tokens you never see as output. The sheer range the buyer has to choose from is why the buying desk in the next section exists.

The Buying Desk: Choosing a Provider on Every Call

When the price of the same product spans more than eight times, sending every call to the top-tier model is no different from booking every business trip in first class. So a new layer wedged itself between the application and the model, one that decides automatically, each time a call happens, which factory this batch of tokens comes from. Sitting above the layers described so far, it deserves the name buying desk.

That automatic choice brings risk along with convenience, as OpenAI’s GPT-5 launch in August 2025 demonstrated. OpenAI turned on a real-time router in ChatGPT by default, sending easy queries to a small model and hard ones to a large model, while in the API users still picked among three model sizes themselves [7]. The early routing didn’t work properly, complex queries flowed to the small model, and when the backlash framed the new model as a regression, the company responded by restoring the previous model as an option [8]. What happened first in a consumer product can repeat exactly in an enterprise buying managed routing: when you don’t know what handled your query, you can neither diagnose a quality drop nor hold anyone accountable for it.

The products that handle both the convenience and the risk fall into three groups. Independent gateways hand the dial between quality and cost to the user. Hyperscalers have absorbed routing as a built-in feature. And model vendors, as with GPT-5, put routing inside the product so the consumer buys a quality tier of the result rather than a model. The first two groups appear below, in the OpenRouter section and in the AWS Bedrock and Microsoft Foundry sections.

While the market has been selling routing as a product, academia has been working the same problem in the open. From FrugalGPT (2023), which first formalized the cascade approach of trying the cheap model first, through to LLMRouter in 2026, individual techniques passed through standard benchmarks and converged into unified libraries. LLMRouter’s report that a trained router scored 14.6% better in relative terms than the single strongest fixed model [9] means something beyond cost: because different models are good at different queries, a combination can beat the single best one. Routing is a standardizable learning problem rather than one vendor’s secret, and it is a capability an organization can bring in-house by training on its own query distribution and its own quality judgments on top of it.

While the routing technology was being commoditized that way, the seat at the buying desk became more expensive. Palo Alto Networks acquired the gateway startup Portkey and made it the control point of its security platform [10], and in August 2026 Stripe agreed to acquire OpenRouter [11], at a price reported above $7 billion — more than five times the $1.3 billion valuation it carried three months earlier [24]. The price of routing is being set on the seat, not on the technology.

Which leaves one question. If demand can move between factories instantly at the flip of a switch, and even the technology that builds the switch is open source, what is left for the side that makes and sells tokens? The six vendors in the next chapter are each answering that question in their own way.

The Vendors, One by One

The six major players are examined in order of their position in the value chain: independent inference clouds, the dedicated-silicon camp, the buying desk, and the hyperscalers. The focus is on what each one offers as a moat against the pressure of commoditization.

Fireworks AI: Putting a Price on Speed

Fireworks AI, an independent inference cloud, is the most successful case of making speed the moat. The company says annual recurring revenue passed $1 billion in July 2026 and that it processes more than 40 trillion tokens a day [12]. A customer list including Cursor, Notion, Quora, and Vercel shows where Fireworks sits [13]: code editors and document tools, companies where the time a user spends waiting for a response is the product’s quality.

At the center of the technology is FireAttention, an attention kernel the company built itself, with a stack of optimizations on top that raise token output from the same GPU. Fireworks has said the FP8 implementation of this kernel runs four times faster than the open-source engine vLLM [14], and its customer Notion says it cut response latency from about two seconds to 350 milliseconds [13]. Beyond serverless it offers dedicated endpoints and on-premises deployment, covering all three purchase modes, and in 2026 it began being offered as a first-class service inside Microsoft Foundry — a rare case of an independent inference cloud moving inside a hyperscaler’s contract and invoice.

Where it hurts. Speed carries a price. Fireworks’ serverless rate for models above 16B is $0.90 per million tokens, which next to the cheapest provider’s $0.12 is 7.5 times more [15][6]. For a workload that doesn’t need speed, like a batch summarization job running overnight, that is overkill, and the abundance of quantization levels and caching options means finding the right combination costs you learning time as well. Fast tokens have not become a commodity yet — that is Fireworks’ argument, and it holds only for workloads where response speed is product quality.

Together AI: When the Department Store Starts Buying Factories

Together AI positions itself as the department store of the open-source model ecosystem. It leads with being far cheaper than closed APIs, and on top of a catalog serving hundreds of open models it runs a spectrum that extends into fine-tuning services and dedicated GPU clusters. The company reported a backlog of $1.15 billion as of July 2026 [16], and in August said it was processing 400 trillion tokens a month [17].

The number worth noticing in the July 2026 funding announcement is a different one: the 500 megawatts of committed compute Together said it had secured — the industry counts compute capacity in power [18]. Because the margin in the upper layer is protected only by utilization, Together went down the value chain to grab the source of utilization, which is supply. In August 2026 it signed a multi-year agreement worth $240 million to place dedicated inference GPU clusters on IBM Cloud [17], which amounts to renting someone else’s factory as well. What Together sells is economies of scale and fine-tuning, and the flow of comparing models in a playground and then starting a fine-tuning job from the same screen is a genuine strength of the experience.

Where it hurts. Pressure comes from above and below at once. From above, there is no closed frontier model, so verifying that open models are good enough falls to the customer. From below, operators running their own GPUs compete on raw price, and measured open model against open model, Together sits on the expensive side. The company’s claim of being six to twenty times cheaper is a comparison against closed models [18].

Groq and Cerebras: Speed You Can’t Get From a GPU

What these two companies, which make tokens on dedicated silicon rather than GPUs, put forward is not cluster-wide throughput but the speed a single request feels: time to first token, and generation speed after it. Producing each token requires reading the entire model weights out of memory, so the generation phase is bound by memory bandwidth rather than compute, and both companies sidestep that bottleneck by holding weights in on-chip SRAM instead of the GPU’s external memory (HBM). Groq’s LPU has a structure where execution order is fixed in advance and nothing waits, specialized for small and mid-size models; on independent benchmarks it generates a widely used 70B-class open model at over 300 tokens per second, well ahead of GPU-based providers [6]. Real-time agents and voice conversation — workloads where a few hundred milliseconds of delay is product failure — are Groq’s territory.

Cerebras applies the same principle on a far larger chip. The WSE-3, which uses an entire wafer as one chip, carries hundreds of times more SRAM than a GPU’s on-chip memory and generates over 900 tokens per second even on 405B-class models. The company listed on Nasdaq in May 2026, raising around $5.5 billion [19]; in second-quarter results announced in August it said cloud segment revenue had grown nearly fourfold year over year [20]; and it signed a multi-year supply agreement with OpenAI worth more than $20 billion at 750 megawatts [21].

Where it hurts. Both pay the same price. Because weights have to fit in SRAM, chip count climbs steeply with model size, every new model costs porting work, and model coverage is narrower than at GPU providers, with recurring stretches where supply can’t keep up with demand. Still, there is a speed no amount of dial-turning gets out of a GPU, and the party that put the highest price on that speed was a GPU company itself: in December 2025 NVIDIA bought a non-exclusive license to Groq’s inference technology, in a deal reported at $20 billion, and took on key people including founder Jonathan Ross, while Groq remains an independent company and continues to run GroqCloud [22].

OpenRouter: A Buying Desk Bought by a Payments Company

OpenRouter is the only one of the six vendors with no GPUs. It gathers more than 400 models and more than 80 providers behind a single OpenAI-compatible API, passes the provider’s token price straight through, and adds a 5.5% fee when you buy credits [23]. It is not a factory but the canonical buying desk, and as of May 2026 it had 8 million users and was processing 100 trillion tokens a month, a fivefold increase in six months [24]. Then in August 2026, the payments company Stripe agreed to buy that buying desk [11].

The core features are breadth of model choice, fallback that automatically moves to another provider when one fails, and an auto router that hands the quality-versus-cost dial to the user. Standing at the buying desk also means seeing the whole market, which is an asset only OpenRouter has. By the usage statistics the company publishes, programming workloads grew from 11% of all tokens in early 2025 to more than half by year end [25], and in February 2026 Chinese models passed American ones in weekly token throughput for the first time, accounting for 46% of routed tokens as of June [26].

Where it hurts. The weaknesses of the position remain. Customers whose usage grows want to contract directly with providers to get volume discounts and committed rates, routing technology is open source and can be built in-house, and enterprise governance features like audit logs and budget controls are still maturing. So what did Stripe buy? Half the answer is that OpenRouter’s fee attaches at the moment of payment, and the other half is in what Stripe’s Patrick Collison said announcing the deal, that tokens are the central currency of companies building products with AI [11]. OpenRouter’s founder called the company “the Stripe of AI.” What Stripe bought is less the routing technology than the checkout counter where 8 million users pay for tokens, and the meaning of the deal is that the buying desk’s seat costs more than the technology standing on it.

AWS Bedrock: The Contract You Already Signed

Where an earlier report covered Bedrock AgentCore as agent execution infrastructure, what we look at here is the layer beneath it in the same Bedrock: the one that sells tokens. AWS’s managed inference service offers hundreds of foundation models through a single API [27], and it reaches the enterprise through contracts a great many organizations have already signed. Buying tokens inside an AWS contract you have already signed, with IAM permissions, VPC boundaries, and compliance certifications — inside a security perimeter that has already cleared review — means procurement and security get to skip the whole process of vetting a new vendor.

In purchase-mode terms, Bedrock handles all three within one product. On-demand bills per token; batch inference accepts latency in exchange for the industry-standard half price; and provisioned throughput commits to a capacity that, if you fill it, saves 30 to 50%. That lets you follow the utilization argument above by changing rate plans rather than changing vendors. Routing has been absorbed too: Intelligent Prompt Routing, generally available since April 2025, predicts response quality per request and moves between differently sized models within the same family, and AWS reports internal testing showing cost savings of 35% for the Nova family and 56% for the Anthropic family [28].

Where it hurts. The price of staying inside a perimeter that has already cleared review is a narrower set of choices. New models reach the Bedrock catalog on a lag after they hit the market; routing works only within a family, so crossing families is still a human decision; and because per-model prices are fixed at AWS list, the eight-times spread between providers never enters the perimeter. What holds you to Bedrock is the contract you already signed rather than the price of the tokens, and from a buyer’s standpoint that is exactly the kind of convenience to watch most closely.

Microsoft Foundry: Azure as a Distribution Network

Microsoft Foundry was renamed from Azure AI Foundry in January 2026, moving up from one Azure service to a core Microsoft product [29]. What we look at here is the inference layer of the same Foundry the earlier report covered as an agent platform: a catalog where you can pick not only OpenAI models but Anthropic’s Claude, DeepSeek, xAI, Meta, and Mistral [30].

What stands out strategically is the rearrangement of distribution. Fireworks being folded into Foundry is a new distribution channel for Fireworks, and for Foundry it is a way to put open models like DeepSeek and Qwen in the catalog without running the serving infrastructure itself. Routing is in place as well: as of 2026, Model Router puts OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Meta, and xAI models behind a single deployment and lets the operator choose a balance, cost, or quality strategy, a wider range of choice than Bedrock’s within-family routing. The router itself, though, carries a separate charge based on input tokens [31].

Where it hurts. On the user’s side, existing Azure OpenAI endpoints and Foundry endpoints coexist, and which one to use remains confusing. Billing splits by model and by deployment mode and is complicated; in August 2026 Microsoft stopped per-token billing for some open models, leaving only provisioned throughput [32]. The convenience Bedrock gives an organization settled on AWS, Foundry gives one settled on Azure, but for an organization running both clouds it is one more product family to manage. What holds you to Foundry even so is having the agent platform and the token store in one console.

A Closing Note for CIOs and CTOs

What a buyer needs in the token economy is not a particular vendor but purchasing capability. No vendor will solve, on your behalf, the condition of not knowing what you use and how much. Four recommendations follow.

1. Start by measuring what you use and how much

More than half of AI infrastructure spending is inference, so measure token spend by model, by team, and by workload as an organizational practice. Which vendor is cheaper is the second question. The first is what queries we send, how many, and what share of them genuinely require the top-tier model. The first action is to make a list of how many LLM API keys have been issued inside the organization and which team uses each for what. If keys aren’t separated by team and purpose, no dashboard will ever show you spend by team. On top of that, most of the tokens a repetitive agent consumes are input tokens re-reading the same context, so the hit rate of prompt caching — where cached tokens are billed at roughly a tenth of list price — moves cost more than the price sheet does.

Buyers in Korea have further items to count. First, the same content sent in Korean costs more tokens than in English, and the multiplier differs by each model’s tokenizer, so a model that looks cheap on the price sheet can be the expensive one for a Korean-language workload. Second, prices are set in dollars, so a won-denominated budget moves with the exchange rate. Third, the lag before a new model is listed in the domestic region, and the data-export judgment involved in using an overseas region, are items to check before signing. Pinning a region carries an extra cost: AWS prices a request that leaves processing open to the whole world about 10% below one confined to a particular geography [33].

This measuring is not only a private-sector matter. In the public sector, whether tokens count as consumables or a service subscription changes both budget formulation and contracting method. Per-token billing follows the logic of a consumables budget, provisioned throughput the logic of a service subscription, and which way the statement of work is written determines the bidding unit and the settlement method. Without measurement you can’t even tell which logic fits your own workload.

2. Decide the purchase mode workload by workload

In orders of magnitude, GPU utilization around 40 to 50% is the boundary between serverless and dedicated, and self-hosting has to be calculated on GPU cost plus staff plus spare capacity. Send predictable large workloads down a layer and keep exploratory ones on serverless — but make the decision per workload. A good first action is to pick the single workload with the largest token spend, plot calls by hour, and gauge the ratio of peak to average; then use that ratio to put your own organization’s break-even into numbers. If the graph is flat, getting one quote for a committed rate or a dedicated endpoint is the right move. Mature organizations run all three modes at once.

3. Measure and evaluate before you route

Buy routing without an evaluation framework built on your own workloads and you get the enterprise edition of the GPT-5 backlash: quality drops and nobody knows why. A routing policy only means something after measurement tells you what you use, and after evaluating models against your own queries tells you which tier is sufficient for which task. The first action is to pull the queries you actually run, put the answers from a higher-tier and a lower-tier model side by side, and have the people who own the work score them into an evaluation set. Model selection has moved from a one-time procurement decision to an operating variable that has to be re-decided continuously, and maintaining that evaluation set is something the senior owners need to keep attending to.

4. Stay in a state where you can switch vendors

Being able to switch vendors is itself negotiating power. When vendors say you can move by changing only the endpoint address, thanks to OpenAI-compatible APIs, that means it is easy to come in — and equally easy to leave. Validating open models yourself and using a buying desk are both tools that lower switching costs. And as Stripe’s acquisition of OpenRouter shows, the buying desk can become someone else’s property too, so keeping the gateway itself open source belongs on the list of options. The first action is a switching rehearsal: move one primary workload to another provider’s endpoint and run it for a day. That record becomes your evidence, at contract renewal, that you can leave. The factories know this too, of course, and manufacturing reasons you can’t leave is their job. Those circumstances are the subject of the next report.

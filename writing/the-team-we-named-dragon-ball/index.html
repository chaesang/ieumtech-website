The Team We Named Dragon Ball

Toriyama Akira died. I remembered how Dragon Ball carried me — the small talk that leaped my English, and the team it once named at Google.

When I saw the news that Toriyama Akira, the creator of Dragon Ball, had passed away, I sat with it for a while — a fan who had lived through the same era, wanting to send some gratitude along with the grief. I’d wrestled, like a lot of people, with whether to keep following the story after the Majin Buu arc. But Dragon Ball, alongside Slam Dunk, is stitched into a part of my past, and I’m grateful for that. It also did me some quiet favors during the years I spent at Google, through my thirties and forties. So I want to leave a few lines here, and hope the thanks find their way to him.

Talking with the Japanese team

On a handful of projects I got to work with friends at Google Japan. Back then Japan had a much higher search share than Korea and had long been the hub for Asia-wide engineering, so a lot happened there — projects, workshops, plenty to talk about. I could read hiragana, katakana, and some kanji, and anime had left me with enough light Japanese to get by. But at the company the default was English.

For someone who never studied abroad and didn’t start English until past thirty, progress was slow. Even now I can just about say what I need to and catch what I want to catch. Getting even this far took three quantum jumps, and the first of them is a conversation with these Japanese friends. Small talk and plain confidence account for more of a language than people admit. I still remember getting close to those friends over two hours of talking about Dragon Ball, in English. Some of them spoke better English than I did, some stumbled about as much as I did — but was there ever a better topic than this for trading words with someone, nothing political anywhere near it? Slam Dunk, for the record, was hard to discuss: in Korea the characters had all been given Korean names, so we couldn’t line them up. Evangelion split people. Rurouni Kenshin, a period piece, felt a little awkward to bring up. One of the three great leaps my English ever made, I owe to Dragon Ball.

The project called Dragon Ball

In 2015, Google’s US headquarters proposed — possibly for the first time — a project to build a product just for one region. Then as now, Korea and Japan were unusual: for the Play Store, unlike Google’s other products, they sat in the top three countries, and developers hoping to go global wanted to keep a good relationship with both. But under a global-first doctrine, nothing separate had ever been done for them. The proposal was to build a Play Store product specifically for the Korean and Japanese markets, and it pulled together a product team, resident at US headquarters, made up of people who could speak Korean or Japanese. We got headcount for one product manager, one UX designer, and eight software engineers, on the condition that the whole thing run for two years and then wind down. I took the engineering lead for the effort, and what today would be called the PO role on the Korea side.

There’s plenty of historical tension between the two countries. But from where the product sat, the work asked us to set that tension aside and focus on the products — to hold Korean users and Korean developers, Japanese users and Japanese developers, all in the same frame. Gathering people who spoke Korean or Japanese to run this out of the US, the name came almost on its own: Dragon Ball. Nobody objected; the decision was easy, and for two years we were the Dragon Ball team. The manga was famous enough in the US that sponsors at headquarters understood it instantly, and I’m fairly sure our team intro slides had a photo of us doing the Kamehameha pose.

In a global company, team names and project names get spent on all sorts of things, and then disappear, and explaining them back and forth burns a surprising amount of energy. Against all that, this was a wise choice — one more good memory.

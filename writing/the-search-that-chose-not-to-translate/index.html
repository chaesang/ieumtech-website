The Search That Chose Not to Translate

Once translation stops working as an excuse, the question moves to what you built in that language in the first place.

The tenth piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

The last piece was about filling the strings on a screen. This one is about a fork that sits earlier: whether to translate the documents themselves. And Search, which handles the world’s documents, and a store, which handles what customers upload with their own hands, had very different things to weigh.

At that fork, asked whether automatic translation was allowed, the two products answered in opposite directions. Take the Play Store first. A listing page breaks into discrete fields, and even counting the whole world there were at most a million apps worth having. An app is also something a developer uploads by hand, so the moment it arrives it has already paid an indexing cost once, which meant layering automatic translation on top did not make the critical path any heavier. Search was a different scale of problem entirely. It handles the world’s documents, and you cannot know in advance which country or language a user will arrive from, so preparing every document in more than forty languages was never even thinkable. So Search made no attempt to translate documents at index time at all.

That the Store could switch automatic translation on did not mean it should. The moment you serve a machine translation, the quality of that output becomes the platform’s to answer for. Whether the uploader’s intent survives in it was its own question. If a carefully written description gets clumsily machine-translated without permission and the original author loses by it, whose fault is that. So the platform had more to watch than it looked from outside, and some part of the operating fee taken from sellers quietly went into watching exactly this.

Instead of translating documents in advance, Search took the other path: receive the question, hop the language barrier, and go fetch the answer. CLIR, cross-language information retrieval, as it was called then. You rendered the query in English, searched in English, then brought the results back into Korean, several steps in sequence. It was a project Google Korea put real weight behind in the early days. It meant inserting translation into the critical path more than once, and then holding the documents you had crossed over to collect against what a native-language search would have returned, so even finding the query sets where the approach plausibly worked was no small task. On top of that, the user had to wait while you crossed all those bridges.

Machine translation in that era got better the more it had of what we called golden translations, the same content lined up side by side across languages. This being Google, English sat at the center of all of it, so the better a language correlated with English the smoother its translation ran, and anything that was not English generally went through English in two steps. Korean to Japanese was weighed each time between going direct and going through English, and that second route lost points at the very first step, rendering Korean into English, with meaning shaved down again on the second crossing. Korean and Japanese sit close in word order and in their shared Chinese-derived vocabulary, so connecting them directly should have been better, but there was not enough golden data for that either, and not enough good data made a convenient excuse for quality that would not rise.

What seeds those golden translations is material that has been organized and placed somewhere public, Wikipedia or the UN’s official documents, which skews overwhelmingly English. Korean had far less of it stacked up in parallel, even measured against Japanese. The reason Korean kept falling behind in translation had less to do with how many people speak it than with not being able to draw freely on good public material for good translation to lean on.

What took down that English-centered wall was Google’s neural machine translation, GNMT. Holding many languages in one model and planting them in a shared space moved the work closer to what the words mean than to swapping surface tokens. Going from Korean straight to Japanese without passing through English became natural, and the meaning lost across repeated crossings fell away with it. Putting languages in one space carried forward into models like BERT, and on into today’s LLMs, which now search across material in several languages together without a translation step at all. This is also my memory of a season when the translation team, a group with real history and standing inside Google, tore up the logic it had built over years and rewrote it. Remarkable people, thinking back on it.

The thing we weighed for so long has quietly stopped being a question. Translation no longer works as an excuse. As the machinery got good, what remains moves toward what you built in that language in the first place and what you left open. The lower the language barrier falls, the more an answer to a Korean question comes back leaning on what was stacked up in English, which means the question of where that answer is standing may only be starting now.

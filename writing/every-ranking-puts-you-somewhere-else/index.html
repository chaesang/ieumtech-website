Every Ranking Puts You Somewhere Else

Population, speakers, documents on the web. Korea's place moved with every line it stood in, and those numbers decided the budget.

The fourth piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

Working on the Google Search team, I covered Korea and Korean, but naturally the English-speaking user on the U.S. West Coast came first. When someone said “users,” the 300 million in the U.S. were what came to mind, and carrying something over from Korea meant meeting that reference point with 50 million in hand before anything else. Fifty million assumed 100% share, so even that was generous.

Lift the numbers a little, though, and there isn’t one way to line countries up. Rank by population and India and China stand at the front with 1.4 billion each while the U.S. sits well back at 300 million. Rank languages by how many people speak them and English stands first at 1.5 billion, though fewer than 400 million speak it as a first language and the rest learned it second. Rank the documents on the web by language and English takes close to half, followed by Spanish and German and Japanese, while Chinese, spoken by 1.4 billion, barely clears 1%. Population order, language order, and document order each stood in a different line. Share figures multiplied into all of it along with various strategies, and these are mixed memories, so treat the figures and rankings as approximate.

Here language and country get tangled once more. Because a locale is by nature one tag binding a language to a country, even inside the U.S. the people using English and the people using Spanish stood in separate lines as en_US and es_US, each a meaningful block of its own. There really are a great many people, in California especially, who speak only Spanish and no English, and es_US ranked as one of the largest Spanish locales after es_MX. Cross to Europe and countries with a firm language of their own, Germany or France, still have a substantial layer of English laid over the top. One country, one language rarely balanced out cleanly.

English dominated not only in people but more so in the volume of content piled onto the web, and beneath it the Latin languages sat heavily in several senses, with Spanish and Portuguese giving Brazil and Mexico numbers that were nothing to dismiss, and Southeast Asian figures just starting to climb around then. In that gap, Korean ranked somewhere around tenth in the world by number of speakers but dropped to a place that didn’t register at 1% when counted by content open on the web. It wasn’t that Korean content didn’t exist. The good material sat in private territory, invisible to anyone counting from outside. Machine translation wasn’t what it is now, and paired documents carrying the same content in several languages were rare, so I’ll come back to that when the series reaches translation and content.

What makes English formidable isn’t just size. Even en_US, one of the largest single locales at 250 million, is only about five times ko_KR’s 50 million. But because English is the hub every translation passes through and the reference language for search and for learning, the gap you felt whenever you compared search quality or document quality ran much wider than that. The distance to first place was always farther than the numbers said.

Take Korea alone and change the line it stands in, and the position swings every time. Around tenth in the world by GDP, outside the top thirty by population, and off the chart entirely by web content. Being roughly tenth by language was an awkward place to invest from and an awkward place to cut. Not high enough that touching it brought large numbers along immediately, not small enough to be worth folding away. The priority always landed a beat late. We tried using the low share against 50 million as an argument that there was room to grow, but apply that low share and Korea’s standing against other countries inside Google came back as a worse report card.

Covering Korea meant looking often at other countries and languages. For a while China, Japan, and Korea, with Hong Kong added, were grouped as CJK or CJHK and worked together out of shared circumstance. The ambition was fine, but how was that going to work. China fell out of search at some point, and Taiwan and Hong Kong were still before proper offices had been established, so seeing it through together was difficult. Japan went the other way: share around 40% until it merged with Yahoo Japan search and passed 90%, which is a different sort of departure. Korea was still four or five years away from Android phones and YouTube breaking through, so it was a stretch of fighting alone.

Numbers were certainly the starting point of every discussion, and 300 million in the U.S., 1.4 billion in India and China, 1.5 billion in English were laid out at the top of every meeting. But step one pace further in and a country’s position changed entirely depending on which line you made it stand in, and even those numbers were held separately by each service, which made gathering them in one place for comparison difficult. For a global company allocating investment by country this was a natural and reasonable discussion to have, strategically speaking, though I remember it wearing me down. Given another run at it I might do a little better, he says lightly.

Back when I was in the thick of it, Google had plans for India, Southeast Asia, Latin America, and Africa laid out ahead, and my own fighting ended here. With sovereignty the subject of the day, I recently saw Vietnamese and Indonesian sitting above Korean in CommonCrawl’s document rankings, and it left me with a good deal of regret. I’m not a player anymore, so my position is a little different. Still, before we reduce digital sovereignty to one measure of country and language, I worry about whether enough good Korean, enough good Korean documents, are visible in the places where AI and search engines are looking.

Jung, Jeong, or Chung

The romanization of a Korean surname — Jung, Jeong, Chung — and a lifetime of being pronounced, and mispronounced, across borders.

A disclaimer: this one is steeped in Korean and Sino-Korean naming culture — surnames written in Chinese characters, and the long puzzle of turning them into the Roman alphabet.

vs. the Jeong of 鄭

The surname Jeong used in Korea splits, at roughly nine to one, between the 鄭 Jeong and the 丁 Jeong, and my family uses the 丁 — the “wooden rake” Jeong. Names each have their story, but for a generation that wrote its names in Chinese characters from birth, a simple-looking one was a real advantage, and what a “wooden rake” even was, I only learned much later. My grandfather said the two Jeongs differed in pronunciation and tone, but that sort of thing I mostly let drift past. On one side the representative figures are the Jeong Yak-yong brothers, on the other Jeong Mongju — but oddly, not many of us turn up famous in the history books.

The first time I thought about my name in English — in the Roman alphabet — was when I first entered university, and it hardened when I got a passport during my working years. Unconsciously I chose “Jung,” and it’s carried through to now. I remember six classmates entering under the same surname the same year, though I can only picture five, so maybe it was five. That there were three variations I learned fairly late, and that I got to choose one past the age of twenty is, thinking about it now, a strange thing.

vs. “Yung”

At a global company, US Google, there were people from many countries with all sorts of complicated names, next to which Korean names were incomparably simple. The moments when people who’d worked together by email met face to face and, wanting to do it right, asked each other’s names with kind eyes and practiced the pronunciation — those are among my more precious memories. It matters more than I first thought. One scene I remember around this: the way the boss of the organization, at a public recognition like a promotion announcement, would call out names with care. I even got the homework of asking the managers of the people to be mentioned to record and send the pronunciations, and the way he’d thank you with a smile, then greet each person crisply, eye to eye, was one of the cultures I most wanted to learn.

Naturally, to friends from Europe I said quite a lot that it’s not “Yung” but “Jeong” — since there, Jung reads like the German name. On a trip to Switzerland in particular, there was a fairly famous local bakery chain called Jung, and I flinched more than once at the street signs. It was around then that I thought hard about whether I should have gone with “Chung” — but to change all that complicated paperwork…

vs. Jeong, vs. Chung

Very late, I came to find that my family members use different spellings. Sorting my father’s belongings — he’d been a seaman, often crossing borders — I found he’d used two by turns, and my younger sibling and a cousin use different ones. Each of us probably first had to think about it when making our own passport, but of course we never consulted one another. Because of this, immigrating to the US brought a little difficulty with family-relationship proofs, extra translation documents, notes to the effect of the English names differ but it’s actually the same surname.

I have no wish now to rank the three words. As I learned later, figures who became famous in earlier generations — Chairman Chung Ju-yung of Hyundai, the conductor Chung Myung-whun — used “Chung,” while among athletes and K-pop idols these days you do see “Jung” too. It makes me oddly glad.

ps. First name

I’d considered a separate English first name at times, but somehow missed the plausible timing. At Starbucks in the US I started just writing “John.” I tried “Jung,” but it was hard — though from the moment you order by app these days, they start calling my name again, hard as it is. I once considered “Charles,” close to the Korean pronunciation. But the moment the entertainers and politicians using that name got dragged through the gossip, I thought, not this — and Charles is about as hard as Chaesang anyway. The friends I’ve met in the US do pronounce it well enough. It’s closer to “Chai-sang” than “Chaesang,” and my Chinese and Japanese friends call me “Chaesan.” At that level there’s no trouble at all, and I’m grateful.

A Crawler Has a Country, Too

Most of the web is no longer human traffic. The question of where a bot calls from is older than the AI crawlers now fighting over it.

The seventh piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

Spread out the world’s web traffic and look at it, and the share that is not human comes as a surprise. One industry report put automated traffic past 53% of the web in 2025, up from 51% the year before. Not all of it is the same kind of bot. Some, like a search engine’s crawler, go around announcing who they are and what they want. Others, a DDoS flood among them, have to be turned away at the door.

For a long time a bot mostly meant crawl, a search engine going around reading the world’s documents in advance so it would have them ready. Now fetch is growing fast: someone asks a question, and the web gets searched on the spot to put an answer together. That is what LLM services are doing when they go looking while a user waits. Break AI crawling down by purpose and the shape is clear enough. In Cloudflare’s accounting for the third quarter of 2026, close to half of AI crawler requests, 47.6%, were collection for model training, 8.3% were for search indexing, a little under 40% were crawls with mixed purposes, and the fetches that actually arrive to answer a person’s question came to 3.7%. Scraping and answering will both keep growing for a while yet. What strikes me is the patience. In the old search, a result a beat late was a crisis. Now people wait, willingly, while something runs a live fetch on their behalf.

Asking where a bot comes from did not start with LLMs. When I was running Korean search, around the time google.co.kr and google.com were being folded into one, the question was already there. How far should the same query from the same person diverge depending on whether they are sitting in Korea or in the United States. Near the top of the priority list was a rule that sounds almost too plain. Click the result and you must not land on a not found or a not allowed. Content behind a wall, a post that was never set to public, a site that geo-blocks Korean visitors outright: we wanted those kept out of the results at roughly the same level as a dead link.

Geo-cloaking, where the same URL glances at where you are connecting from and hands you a different page, turned out to be more common than you would think. Hand a Korean user a page crawled in the United States and what they actually meet on the click can be a different thing entirely. So in principle the results served to Korean users had to come from a crawler that ran in Korea. For sites that treat every country alike it makes no difference. For sites with logic inside, the same Google crawler was treated one way arriving from the United States and another arriving from Korea, and the answer we settled on was to run crawlers per country. It was resources, which meant it was an investment, which meant rounds of persuasion, and there were plenty of moments where we came up short.

For a stretch my job was finding the good Korean sites and securing crawl quota one at a time, and most of what I remember is how hard it was. Back then the assumption that a foreign company was carting off Korean content sat even on sites that were already public, so knocking was delicate from the start. Search is not advertising. You do not pay for the slot; the value gets set only when a user chooses to click. That made the question of which sites to include heavier than it looks. And Korean sites often did not follow web standards closely enough, so a crawler reading what was inside them was not even straightforward.

Sites have their own reasons for opening the door selectively. A U.S. insurance site blocking connections from Korea, Netflix showing only what is licensed in each country: that is licensing and regulation doing its work. Turn the same gating around and apply it to public data, or to something you actually want known, and the story changes. We paid for this with our taxes, so foreigners should not see it. In an age when crawlers are how the world gets read, that comes close to barring your own gate. Not that you can crawl every site from every country. Which sites are worth the effort and the traffic was its own weighing, and it tangled naturally into the judgment of whether a site was worth it at all, which is to say site quality.

And once a name starts getting known, crawlers and bots from who knows where hammer at the door. The very crawl that is priceless to a search engine comes back to the site owner as maintenance cost and operational burden. Recent measurement bears it out. Fastly found that in January 2026 bots made up 49% of requests against humans at 51%, that only 1% of those bots were verified or wanted, and that 60% of all origin traffic came from bots. Cloudflare mitigated 27.8 million DDoS attacks in the first half of 2025 alone, 130% of the total for all of 2024, with hyper-volumetric attacks averaging 71 a day. The same traffic is a road to the world for one party and a bill to be paid by another.

SEO and web standards were a wall back then, hard for most people to clear, and in places a matter of tricks. With AI putting a standards-compliant page within anyone’s reach, that wall is neither a wall nor a trick anymore, and the names making the rounds in its place are AEO and GEO, answer engine and generative engine optimization. Looking back, the wariness Korean sites held toward Google’s crawler was a trailer for the fight the whole world is now having over crawlers that collect training data. What sites expect has probably not moved. What I regret is that in a season when sovereignty is the subject of the day, opening information up still gets counted as letting it leak abroad.

Now that bots travel the web more than people do, and the bots themselves split into crawl and fetch, that old question of where a crawler comes from and what it takes has only gotten heavier. When an AI builds its answer out of pages it fetched on the spot, and one of those pages cannot be reached from where the person asking is standing, whose world is that answer a view of. I have been away from traditional search a long while now. I still find myself wondering who is carrying the old rule, the one that said if you click it, it has to be there.

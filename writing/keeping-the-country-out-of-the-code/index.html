Keeping the Country Out of the Code

A branch written for one country is a debt every other user helps pay down. So we kept the country in config instead of in the code.

The eighth piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

Build a service that goes out to the whole world, search or a store, and sooner or later the sentence we treat each country differently comes down to how you write one line of code. Where you draw a country’s edge in that line, and how you handle it, turns out to be how the service sees the world.

The easy temptation is to write Korea into the code itself. If country is Korea, do this. But the moment you drop a branch for Korea alone into the middle of a path every user in the world walks through, the cost of that one simple comparison gets split among the 99% and more who have nothing to do with it. A handling for the few, paid for by the many, which is a Pareto of its own running the wrong way. It gets much worse once you reach locale-aware scoring, where each country carries its own weights. The maintenance side was no better. Google’s engineers worked inside a culture that treated code itself as a kind of debt, so everyone knew each of those branches was a balance to be paid down for years afterward. The strict and varied review process says as much.

It is not that such branches never existed. There was a good deal of if China in the code. The Chinese market had reasons, more than a few, and as each reason got its accommodation the branches kept multiplying. When Google withdrew from China, what surfaced in the reckoning was that all that accumulated if China was an awkward load, technically and in terms of the SDLC both, and development teams fought the latent code debt for a long while on either side of it. I watched that from fairly close up. With a case nowhere near China’s weight behind it, an argument for adding if Korea was short on standing before it started.

So the preferred shape was not if Korea but if this feature is on for this country. Rather than lifting Korea out for special treatment, you put every country on the same frame and kept the differences in configuration. Korea’s real-name verification, which the law there required, went in not as a country name in the code but as a feature flag: is this feature on for this locale. Which features went to which countries lived in a well-kept config off to the side. The code could then be refactored freely without the behavior drifting out of line. The hope behind it reached further, too. What if this same verification turns up in some other country, or the law changes and it has to be switched on or off differently.

That fastidiousness was not a matter of taste. It came out of a culture that treated merging something into shared code as adding to a commons, with strict review and approval standing in the way. Even a handling meant for one country was treated as a feature launch in its own right rather than an exception slipped in quietly, and even where the law demanded it, how far the effect reached was worked out through experiment and analysis before any of it was folded into the decision. For more on that culture, see Software Engineering at Google, which Google has put online to read for free.

But before you can give someone something fitted to their country, you have to know what country they are in, and a user who has not even signed in leaves you few clues. You guess the language from what the browser sends in its settings, you place them by the IP they connected on, the geo IP, and for a time you read the door they came in through, a domain like google.co.kr. Some of it was conditions laid out as if statements. Some of it was probability models. That is how I remember it.

What is interesting is that the weight of that phrase, a signed-out user, turned over completely with time. Back when Chrome was uncommon and so were smartphones, nearly all the traffic pouring in from Korea came out of PC bangs, the internet cafes the country ran on, and nearly all of it was anonymous. Now, when everyone goes through the day signed in to their own account, what is left in that seat is the small number who deliberately opened an incognito window. The work of Google’s Chrome team and identity team had a doggedness worth respecting, and the few chances I had to work alongside them taught me a great deal.

Once someone signs in, things get easier. Single sign-on, living under one account wherever you go, was not a culture that existed before Chrome, and doing it at the browser level was an ambitious and enormous project even for Google. As it unfolded, Google began attaching probabilities to accounts. Look across what has passed through an account, mail and documents and the rest, and you can set a ratio for language. My personal account sat at roughly seven to three, Korean to English. My work account ran the other way, about two to eight. It was one of the ways a service spanning many products came to understand a user, and language, it turned out, could be had as a probability.

A country could not. The moment payment is involved a country has to be picked, exactly one, and I kept my personal account in Korea where the carrier and the bank were wired in, and my work account in the United States. Moving the personal account’s country to the U.S. after I left turned out to be more involved than I expected, and once I understood that the procedure connects to international cooperation on tracing things like terrorist financing, I decided the intent was worth following even where it was inconvenient. Taxes and payments, when you think about it, are the heaviest measure of which country a person belongs to. Language you can live across, seven to three. In front of money, and taxes above all, a country has to be one.

Domain and IP, account and taxes. Pull every clue together and the line called a country stays a little too blurred to write into code, while users cross it without giving it a thought. The effort to keep the country out of the code was, looking back, a way of admitting that blur. To everyone running a global service, a small measure of respect, and of sympathy.

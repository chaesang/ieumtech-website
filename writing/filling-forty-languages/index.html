Filling Forty Languages

One particle, one blank string. Shipping a product in forty languages meant holding out an empty vessel and hoping it filled in time.

The ninth piece in a series on how country and language were never one and the same.

This is the story of how a single line of text on a screen made its way into forty languages, and of filling the vessel that held it. When you built something at Google, the design generally assumed from the start that it could go out in more than forty languages and to more than a hundred countries. You left the vessel empty, so to speak, so that anything could ship anywhere once the strings were poured in. Which meant that whenever good translation failed to keep up, that became the bottleneck for the product or the feature. This was back when machine translation was nothing like what it is now, and far more of the work passed through human hands.

Every string that went on screen lived in a translation table. Where a name or a number belonged you left a placeholder, and the sentence got assembled around it. What came back was then reviewed by a linguist who knew the language. With more than forty languages to support, a single empty cell anywhere in that table could hold up an entire feature.

Most of those strings were handled as parameterized strings, the sprintf idea: leave a slot, drop a value in, and the sentence completes itself. Korean gives that approach a hard time. Whether a noun ends in a consonant decides whether the particle after it is 이 or 가, so you cannot mechanically staple one particle behind a placeholder. Word order flips against English constantly, too. The template system had no way to carry that final-consonant rule, so what we ended up writing was 이(가) and 을(를), both forms side by side, and when I run into 이(가) somewhere now I feel a small pang. There was a volunteer group inside the company that helped with Korean translation for products, and anyone who drew the short straw and got handed a disclaimer or some other piece of legal text had a rough time of it. Fonts and line breaking were bearable. The day an Arabic PM, working in a script that runs right to left, spotted something and filed a request, that was work of an entirely different kind.

Since people could not touch every string by hand, we also left machine translation switched on, and then forgot about it for long stretches, until some unidentifiable phrase surfaced somewhere on a screen. In screen templates the failure was usually a word rendered in the wrong sense from among synonyms and near-synonyms, and it tended to get caught only when a linguist, or a local employee in a country that language might ship to, reported it.

That covers the strings we made and put on screen, the ones Google took responsibility for translating. Users, of course, have no interest in who translated what. They want it to read naturally. Seen through that eye, even a translated result lands at different levels of satisfaction. At the bottom sits text that was never translated at all, and within that, Chinese characters showing up on a Korean screen raise discomfort immediately. English is comparatively fine, which made me wonder for a long time whether that was prejudice or something else, and we went at it with user interviews for quite a while. In my Play Store days our primary audience was Koreans living in Korea, and since Korea is not a country where mainland Chinese is in common use, Chinese characters rendering on screen was treated as a serious bug in its own right. A step above that are the strings that read like machine translation, obvious at a glance, always posing the question of whether that counts as effort. At the top sits translation someone cared about. All of that, though, is about text we prepared ourselves. What a content provider uploads with their own hands carries their intent, and while the Play Store was promoting automatic translation as a feature to be proud of at the time, you cannot simply translate and display what someone else posted without permission. That is where another story begins.

Promising forty languages is easy. Keeping the promise is not. We held out an empty vessel first and filled it as we went, waiting with a knot in the stomach over whether the pixels would break or the wrong contents would arrive, and quality sat pooled in that gap the whole time. A service can consider a translation done and a user will not necessarily receive it that way, so satisfaction varied from person to person, and gauging that difference stayed with me longer than moving words from one language into another did. I remember, now and then, the reviews from professional linguists and tech writers, which operated on another level.

That wall seems to have mostly come down since LLMs. Watching something that gave us so much trouble pass by now without comment leaves me feeling the distance of the years, and I send a quiet word of encouragement to whoever is out there filling blanks the way I once did.

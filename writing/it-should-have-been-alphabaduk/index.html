It Should Have Been AlphaBaduk

Ten years after AlphaGo and Lee Sedol — the romance of Go learned from books, two moves that chilled me, and a small regret about the name.

It’s been ten years since the match between Google-DeepMind’s AlphaGo and the master Lee Sedol, and various bits of news drift past. Let me put down a few stories around Go.

Go learned from books, and the romance in it

In middle and high school I saved my allowance to buy Go magazines and read them. I never played people much in earnest, but I kept, for a long while, the hobby of following game records — replaying them by hand, move by move. There were the game records in the newspapers, and it was fun to walk through a full record one stone at a time.

Oddly, I was bad at playing actual people. I hated collapsing to one or two cheap tricks, and losing wounded me more than it should. I don’t know my exact rank, but later, on internet Go, I hit 1-dan on some site and stepped away. In the official amateur scale I was probably around 3-4 kyu.

The masters Cho Hunhyun and Lee Changho, who appear in the recent film The Match, were of the same era, and the monthly Go magazines carried a lot of the Japanese games. Since mine was Go learned for show, the stories outside the contests were more fun — Cho Chikun, who played as if his life were on the line; Fujisawa Shuko, who said he only needed to win four games a year; the always-winning Lee Changho and Cho Hunhyun I cheered for out of patriotism. But for show, or for romance, the first name out is Takemiya of the Cosmic Style. The one move I still remember is move 69 in the first Fujitsu Cup final. A shoulder hit against a star point.

In the first-ever world-tournament final, a move that’s in no book? Cosmic Style or not, I wondered whether that was even possible, and the way he crushed White afterward was the highlight too. I didn’t watch it live, but even with my shallow skill I remember wondering how such a thing was possible — the most memorable scene, to me, among moves a person has ever played.

Early machine Go

When I joined the computer-engineering department in 1993, a professor told me chess AI — Deep Blue, or Deep Thought — played better than ordinary people, and around then a program called Badajanggi, running on Windows 95, played Korean chess better than most people I knew. By the time I graduated, they said it had beaten the chess champion.

But for Go, even Eunbyeol, said to be the strongest of its day, was rated only around 2-3 kyu — the number of cases is absurdly large, and I spent a long time thinking the intuition people had built up over the ages really was something. In the thick of company life, knowing it only as a kind of pastime, ten years ago that day came.

The AlphaGo match

I was working at Google when there was a big event in Korea, and though I was at headquarters, I’d liked and cheered for the strongest of the age, master Lee Sedol, so I watched with interest — and, in a how dare a mere machine spirit, I bet on the human. The result, as everyone knows, settled into humanity salvaging one final win, and my bet, sad as it was, let’s call it the heart of a supporter.

Two record-breaking scenes. The first is move 37 in game two, a shoulder hit on the fifth line.

By the analyses, after this move there was no chance to turn it around. It touches, in a sense, the example above — and thinking that what we’d call romance when a person does it was a move a machine spat out, I remember it being eerie. As a bonus, from then on I came to think every move AlphaGo stamped out must have a reason. Replaying the records of games one and two, I remember being very afraid.

The next scene is the famous move 78 in game four.

A move in no book, and on the other hand a move hard to believe a person made. Hard to call it a fluke, hard even if you credit deep thought and prediction — and that it was a kind of deliberate bug, exposed by Monte Carlo tree search plus pruning running up against blowing out memory, I now fairly agree with.

As a fan, I’d handed the master I looked up to like an idol, the strongest of his age, the title of humanity’s last winner — but on the other hand I wondered what pain I was handing a man who’d made this his lifelong work, and his decision to retire, and the different life after, I cheer for too.

What I think ten years on

As of 2026 it’s gotten hard to find anything a machine doesn’t do better than a person. That it’s fine teaching material goes without saying, and ten years on it will go into history… but if it had only been AlphaBaduk, I think it would have been a touch better. Go’s Chinese romanization is Weiqi, the Japanese Go, the Korean Baduk — and though the strongest players were Koreans, I felt the regret of having somehow missed the move.

Seeing that Demis Hassabis recently went to Korea to play a special game with the current strongest, master Shin Jinseo, left a bitter taste. He is the father of AlphaGo, true — but an event bringing in a Nobel laureate for a teaching game against the world’s strongest… I have a regret that Google Korea could have paid it a bit more attention.
